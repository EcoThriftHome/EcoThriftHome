Best Home Automation Routines That Save Energy on Autopilot

Set these up once and your bill drops without you doing anything differently. Here's what actually moves the needle — and what's mostly just convenient.

The automation routines that save the most energy are the ones tied to your two biggest loads: heating and cooling, and the standby power your electronics draw around the clock. Everything else — smart lighting, automated blinds, irrigation scheduling — adds something, but those two categories are where real money moves. Set them up correctly and you're done; they run without any further input.

What follows is a ranked-by-impact look at each routine, what hardware you actually need, and realistic savings numbers with the assumptions laid out so you can adjust for your own situation.

1. Thermostat Scheduling (and Geofencing)

This is the biggest lever in home automation, and it's not close. Heating and cooling account for roughly half the average home's energy bill according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration. ENERGY STAR estimates a properly programmed smart thermostat saves about $100–$180 per year, based on setbacks of 7–10°F during the 8 hours you're at work and the 8 hours you're asleep.

A basic schedule handles the sleep setback automatically. Geofencing goes one step further: the thermostat reads your phone's location and sets back when everyone leaves, then starts recovery mode before you arrive home. That's particularly useful for households with irregular schedules where a fixed schedule would either miss savings or leave you coming home to an uncomfortable house.

Before setting up geofencing, check that your thermostat actually supports it and that everyone in the household has the app installed with location permissions on. If one person regularly stays home while others leave, geofencing will fight itself — in that case, a simple schedule is more reliable. We've written a detailed breakdown of how geofencing works and what it's actually worth if you want to dig into the specifics.

Hardware cost: a good smart thermostat runs $80–$130. At $100 savings per year, that's a payback of roughly 10–16 months. Most smart thermostats also give you a usage history, which is useful for catching problems (a sudden spike in runtime often means a dirty filter or a refrigerant issue developing).

2. Standby Power Cutoff for Entertainment and Office Gear

The average U.S. home wastes roughly $100–$200 per year on standby power, sometimes called vampire power. It's not any single device — it's the cumulative draw of a TV, soundbar, game console, cable box, router, desktop computer, monitor, printer, and so on, all sitting in a low-power state 20+ hours a day.

The automation here is simple: plug a smart power strip or a smart plug into your entertainment center or home office setup and schedule it to cut power during hours when no one's home and overnight. For a typical entertainment center drawing 30–50 watts in standby, cutting that for 16 hours a day at $0.16/kWh works out to about $28–$46 per year just from that one outlet cluster. A home office setup with a desktop, monitor, and peripherals can save a similar amount.

One thing to watch: don't put your router on a timed cutoff unless you want to lose remote access and any overnight smart home routines that depend on it. Same goes for a DVR that records overnight. Everything else in those clusters is fair game.

3. Lighting Automations

Lighting is a smaller share of the bill than heating or standby power — usually 10–15% — but it's one of the easiest categories to automate and the savings compound across every room. The three routines that actually save energy (as opposed to just being convenient) are:

Motion-based shutoff for rooms where lights get left on. Bathrooms, laundry rooms, garages, and kids' bedrooms are the usual suspects. A motion sensor switch cuts the light after 5–15 minutes of no movement. At $0.16/kWh and a 10-watt LED bulb left on 4 extra hours a day, that's about $2.30 per year per bulb — not dramatic on its own, but four or five rooms adds up. We've looked at the numbers more carefully in our post on motion sensor light switches.

Sunrise/sunset scheduling for outdoor lights. Leaving outdoor lighting on a fixed timer set in spring means it's running an extra hour in fall when sunset comes earlier. Tying outdoor lights to actual sunrise/sunset times (most smart switches and smart plugs support this) keeps the schedule accurate year-round without you adjusting it manually.

Occupancy-based "all off" routines. If you have a smart home hub, an "everyone's away" routine that shuts off all non-essential lights is worth setting up. It's a catch-all for the light someone forgot before leaving. Even without a hub, a smart plug on a lamp or two on a schedule works fine.

Worth noting: if you haven't switched to LEDs yet, that's the higher-priority step before any automation. A 60-watt equivalent LED uses about 9 watts versus 60 watts for incandescent — that 85% reduction is permanent regardless of scheduling. Automation on top of LEDs is incremental. Automation on incandescents is a band-aid.

4. Water Heater Scheduling

Most tank water heaters run on a simple thermostat: heat the water, hold it at 120°F, reheat when it drops. They don't know you're at work all day or asleep. A smart water heater controller (or a smart plug if you have an electric unit that supports it) can put the heater into setback mode during low-use windows.

The savings here are real but modest. The DOE estimates that setting back a water heater 20°F for 8 hours saves roughly 5–10% on water heating costs. If water heating is running you $400 per year (a rough national average for an electric tank unit), that's $20–$40 in savings. Not the biggest win, but it's a set-and-forget routine on hardware you may already have if your water heater is relatively new.

Before automating this, check two things: whether your water heater has a timer or vacation mode built in already (many newer units do), and whether the unit is electric or gas. Smart controls are straightforward for electric units; gas is more involved and usually not worth the added complexity for the savings available.

5. HVAC Fan and Ventilation Routines

A less obvious one: most smart thermostats let you schedule the HVAC fan independently of heating and cooling. Running the fan for 20 minutes per hour circulates air without running the full conditioning system, which can even out temperature differences between floors and reduce peak cooling load in mild weather.

Whether this saves money depends on your system and climate. If you're in a two-story house where the upstairs runs significantly warmer than the thermostat setting, fan circulation can reduce how often the AC cycles on. If your home is already well-mixed, running the fan more just adds a small cost (a typical HVAC air handler draws 300–500 watts). Check this one against your actual bill before committing to it year-round.

Routines That Aren't Worth Setting Up for Energy Alone

A few automations that show up in "smart home energy" lists but rarely pencil out on energy savings specifically:

Smart refrigerator controls: the fridge runs continuously by design — there's nothing useful to schedule. We'd skip this entirely for energy purposes.

Automated window blinds for solar heat gain: the hardware is expensive ($200–$500+ per window) and the energy payback is very long unless you have a lot of west-facing glass in a hot climate. If you're interested in window treatments for energy, manual cellular shades are far more cost-effective. We've covered that in our post on thermal curtains and cellular shades.

Smart irrigation scheduling: genuinely useful for water and water-heating bills if you have an in-ground system, but the energy savings are indirect. Worth doing for water conservation; just don't expect it to show up dramatically on your electric bill.

Putting It Together: A Realistic Setup

So the three main routines worth prioritizing in order are:

First — thermostat scheduling or geofencing. Biggest impact, clearest payback, nothing else is close.

Second — standby power cutoff on your entertainment center and home office. Use a smart plug with energy monitoring to measure first, then automate the schedule once you know what's actually drawing power.

Third — motion-based or scheduled lighting shutoffs in rooms where lights consistently get left on. Start with one or two rooms, see if the habit is real, then expand.

Each additional routine after those three has diminishing returns. It's worth watching your bill for a couple of months between changes so you can see what's actually working rather than just adding complexity and hoping for the best.

FAQ

Do home automation routines actually save enough to justify the hardware cost?

For the big-ticket routines — thermostat scheduling and standby power control — yes, usually within one to two heating or cooling seasons. A smart thermostat runs $80–$130 and can save $100–$180 per year according to ENERGY STAR. Smart plugs for entertainment centers cost $12–$25 each and typically pay back in under a year on heavily used setups. Lighting automations are closer to break-even unless you're replacing a lot of bulbs.

Which automation saves the most energy?

Thermostat scheduling is almost always the biggest single win. Heating and cooling account for roughly half the average home's energy bill, so even modest setbacks — 7–10°F for 8 hours a day — translate to meaningful savings. Everything else is incremental by comparison.

Can renters use home automation routines?

Most of them, yes. Smart plugs, smart power strips, and plug-in smart thermostats (for window AC or baseboard heaters) require no wiring and no landlord approval. Hardwired smart switches and in-wall thermostats usually need landlord sign-off, though many landlords are fine with it if you agree to restore originals on move-out.

Does geofencing use a lot of data or battery?

Modern smart thermostat geofencing uses very little — most apps are designed to minimize background battery drain by using low-power location fencing rather than continuous GPS polling. In practice, most people don't notice a difference in phone battery life.

Should I automate everything at once?

No. Start with the thermostat, since it has the biggest impact. Then tackle standby power on your entertainment center or home office. Add lighting automations where you consistently forget to turn lights off. Each additional routine has diminishing returns, so it's worth pausing to measure your bill before adding more complexity.

In short: smart thermostat scheduling first, standby power cutoff second, lighting automations third. Measure after each one. That order reflects where the money actually is.
