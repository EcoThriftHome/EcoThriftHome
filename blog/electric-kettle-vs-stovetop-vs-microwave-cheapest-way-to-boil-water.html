Electric Kettle vs Stovetop vs Microwave: Cheapest Way to Boil Water

The energy math is simpler than you'd think. Here's which method costs the least per boil, and why the amount of water you heat matters more than the appliance.

~80%Electric kettle efficiency
~40%Gas burner efficiency
~$3–5Annual savings switching to a kettle

An electric kettle is the cheapest way to boil water in most homes, and it's not particularly close. The gap between a kettle and a microwave is modest, but the gap between both of those and a gas or electric stovetop burner is significant enough to notice over a year of daily use. Here's the reasoning and the numbers behind that.

Why efficiency is the right frame

All three methods convert energy into heat, but they vary a lot in how much of that energy actually ends up in the water versus escaping to the kitchen air. That's the efficiency ratio, and it's the main driver of cost.

An electric kettle heats a coil that sits directly in contact with the water. There's very little wasted heat because there's no gap between the heating element and what you're heating. Most electric kettles transfer roughly 80–85% of the electricity they draw into the water itself.

A microwave works differently — it excites water molecules directly using microwave radiation, which is also fairly efficient. You lose some energy to the container and some to the microwave cavity walls, but not much. Efficiency is typically in the 65–70% range for water, depending on container shape and microwave wattage.

A stovetop burner, whether electric coil, glass ceramic, or gas, has the worst efficiency of the three. An electric coil or radiant burner has to heat the element, then the pot bottom, then the water — losing heat to the air at each step. Gas is worse still because the flame heats the outside of the pot and the surrounding air simultaneously. The U.S. Department of Energy has noted that gas burners are roughly 40% efficient at transferring energy to cookware, while electric resistance burners are around 74%. Induction is higher still (around 84%), but that's a separate post.

The actual numbers per boil

Let's use a concrete example: boiling 1 liter (about 34 oz, enough for two large mugs of tea) from a starting temperature of 65°F to 212°F. We'll use the U.S. average electricity rate of $0.17 per kWh and a natural gas rate of about $1.40 per therm (roughly $0.013 per cubic foot), which are close to EIA mid-2026 averages. Your rates may differ.

The physics: it takes about 0.093 kWh of heat energy to raise 1 liter of water from 65°F to 212°F. That's fixed regardless of appliance. What changes is how much energy you have to put in to deliver that 0.093 kWh of useful heat, given each method's efficiency.

A few notes on the table. The gas cost assumes $0.013 per cubic foot and roughly 1,000 BTU per cubic foot, adjusted for 40% efficiency. Electric stovetop efficiency varies by burner type — glass ceramic runs slightly better than old coil elements; induction would beat the kettle slightly. The microwave wattage matters a lot; a 700W microwave takes longer and isn't necessarily more efficient, just slower.

The annual cost column assumes you boil 1 liter twice a day, every day. That's a fairly heavy tea or coffee household. If you're boiling a single mug (about 300ml) once a day, all the annual figures drop to roughly a quarter of what's shown.

So the savings are small — does it matter?

Honestly, on energy cost alone, the difference between a kettle and a microwave is about $3 a year at that usage rate. That's not the reason to care. The three main reasons the kettle is worth recommending are:

First — speed. A 1,500W kettle boils a liter in about 4–5 minutes. A 1,200W microwave takes 5–7 minutes for the same volume, and you have to watch it to avoid superheating (which is a real and occasionally dangerous issue with microwaved water in smooth containers). The kettle auto-shuts off.

Second — consistency. A kettle brings water to a full rolling boil every time. A microwave often stops before the water is fully boiling, especially at lower power settings, which matters for French press coffee or anything where temperature consistency counts.

Third — habitually boiling only what you need. Kettles with volume markings make it easy to fill exactly one or two cups. That behavioral change — not filling the kettle to the brim for a single mug — is worth more than the appliance switch. Boiling 300ml instead of 1 liter cuts energy use by roughly 70% regardless of appliance.

What about gas vs. electric stovetop?

If you're on a gas stovetop and comparing it to buying a $25 electric kettle, the kettle wins on energy cost at most U.S. electricity rates, even though gas is cheaper per BTU than electricity. The reason: gas burner efficiency is low enough that you're burning a lot of gas for the heat that actually reaches the water. The kettle's higher efficiency more than compensates for electricity's higher per-unit cost in most markets.

That said, if your electricity rate is unusually high (above $0.25/kWh, which is common in Hawaii and parts of California and New England) and your gas rate is low, the math gets closer. Worth checking your actual rates on your bill before assuming the kettle wins by a lot.

You can get more context on what households typically pay on electricity in our post on average electric bills by home size.

The induction exception

If you have an induction cooktop, efficiency climbs to around 84–90%, which actually edges out a standard kettle. But most induction cooktops are paired with large pots, and the convenience factor still favors a dedicated kettle for single-use boiling. Unless you're already using a small induction burner with a proper kettle-sized pot, the stovetop induction advantage is mostly theoretical in daily use.

What to actually do

If you don't have an electric kettle and you boil water regularly for tea, coffee, oatmeal, or anything similar, a basic 1,500W kettle is worth the $20–30 it costs. Not primarily for the energy savings — those are modest — but because it's faster, safer for boiling water than a microwave, and makes it easier to boil only what you need.

If you already have a microwave and only boil water once a day for a single mug, don't stress the comparison. The difference is pennies per day and the microwave is fine.

If you're still using a gas burner out of habit, that's the one worth rethinking. The combination of low gas-to-water efficiency and the time it takes to heat up means the gas stovetop is genuinely the most expensive option for a small boil.

One behavioral note worth repeating: the biggest variable in any of these scenarios isn't the appliance, it's how much water you boil. Filling a kettle to its 1.7-liter maximum for a single cup wastes most of the energy. Fill to the cup line, or measure with your mug first. That change, done consistently, saves more than switching appliances ever will. Frequently asked questions

Does an electric kettle save money compared to a stovetop?

Yes, noticeably so if you're on gas and somewhat so if you're on an electric coil burner. An electric kettle transfers nearly all its energy directly to the water; a stovetop burner loses a lot of heat to the air around the pot. The exact savings depend on your local electricity and gas rates, but the kettle wins in most households.

Is a microwave faster than an electric kettle for boiling water?

For a full mug or more, no. A 1,500W kettle typically beats a 1,200W microwave on time for equivalent volumes, and it's more efficient per liter boiled. The microwave can edge ahead for very small amounts, like 4 ounces or less, and in those cases the cost difference is truly negligible either way.

What if I have a gas stovetop — does the comparison change?

Somewhat, but the kettle still usually wins. Gas is cheaper per BTU than electricity in most U.S. markets, but gas burners are only about 40% efficient at transferring heat to the pot. The electric kettle's roughly 80–85% efficiency often offsets gas's lower unit cost, especially for smaller volumes. At very high electricity rates (above $0.25/kWh), the gap narrows.

Is it worth buying a kettle just to save on boiling water?

Probably not on energy savings alone — the annual difference is a few dollars at most for typical use. But if you make multiple cups of tea or coffee daily, the time savings and convenience are real. A basic electric kettle runs $20–30, and if you use it every day it pays for itself quickly in time saved, if not purely in energy.

Does boiling only the water you need matter more than the appliance choice?

Yes, this is the bigger lever. Boiling half a liter instead of a full liter cuts energy use in half regardless of what appliance you use. The appliance choice is a smaller multiplier on top of that. Use whatever you have, but only boil what you need.

In short: get an electric kettle if you don't have one and you boil water regularly. Keep the habit of filling only what you'll use. And if you're on a gas stovetop, that's genuinely the most expensive option here — moving to an electric kettle for small-volume boiling is the clearest switch to make.
