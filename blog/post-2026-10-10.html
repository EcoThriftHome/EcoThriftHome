A wet basement is more than a comfort issue. It's an energy issue. High humidity forces your HVAC system to work harder, accelerates wood rot and mold, and can quietly cost you hundreds in structural repairs. A well-chosen dehumidifier solves the moisture problem — but a poorly chosen one trades one utility bill problem for another.

This guide focuses on one thing most dehumidifier reviews skip: how much electricity does it actually use, and is that cost justified? We've gathered wattage specs, efficiency ratings, and estimated annual running costs for each model so you can make a genuinely informed decision before you buy.

Key Takeaway: An ENERGY STAR-certified 50-pint dehumidifier costs roughly $85 per year to run at average U.S. electricity rates — about the same as a 60W incandescent bulb left on all day — and prevents mold damage that can cost thousands to remediate.

Why Basement Humidity Is a Bigger Energy Problem Than You Think

Basements are naturally prone to humidity for two reasons: they're below grade (so moisture seeps through concrete), and they have minimal air exchange. When relative humidity (RH) climbs above 60%, mold colonies can establish themselves in as little as 24–48 hours. Beyond mold, high RH air is thermodynamically harder to cool — your air conditioner has to remove that moisture too, which is why a humid basement makes your whole home's cooling system work harder.

"Controlling moisture is the most important factor in preventing mold growth. If moisture problems are not addressed, mold will likely return after cleaning." Maintaining indoor relative humidity between 30% and 50% is the EPA's standard recommendation for inhibiting mold, dust mites, and other biological pollutants.

— U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, A Brief Guide to Mold, Moisture and Your Home

A dehumidifier is the targeted solution. But running the wrong one — either undersized (so it never keeps up) or oversized and inefficient — can add $150–$200 per year to your electricity bill unnecessarily. The sweet spot is an ENERGY STAR-certified unit sized correctly for your basement square footage and moisture level.

How We Evaluated These Models

Every model on this list was evaluated against the following criteria:

  • ENERGY STAR certification: Required. These units meet DOE efficiency standards and use at least 15% less energy than comparable non-certified models.
  • Integrated Energy Factor (IEF): The DOE's primary efficiency metric — liters of water removed per kilowatt-hour (L/kWh). Higher is better.
  • Estimated annual cost: Calculated at $0.17/kWh (2026 U.S. average), 8 hours/day runtime, 180 operating days/year.
  • Humidistat accuracy: Units with accurate built-in humidistats cycle off when the target RH is reached, avoiding wasted runtime.
  • Auto-drain & pump options: Continuous drainage means fewer manual interventions and no overflow shutoffs that leave your basement unprotected.
  • Noise level: Basements are often near living spaces. We note dB ratings where manufacturers publish them.

Dehumidifier Sizing: Get This Right First

Buying the wrong size is the single most expensive mistake. An undersized unit runs continuously without reaching target humidity — wasting electricity and wearing out faster. An oversized unit short-cycles, which is also inefficient and reduces the lifespan of the compressor.

Use this simple sizing guide based on DOE recommendations:

  • 30-pint (17L): Up to 1,500 sq ft, moderately damp (musty smell, no visible moisture)
  • 50-pint (28L): 1,500–2,500 sq ft, wet conditions (visible moisture, wall sweating)
  • 70-pint (40L): Over 2,500 sq ft or very wet (standing water history, seepage)

Note: In 2019 the DOE updated its test conditions to be closer to real-world basement temperatures (65°F vs. the old 80°F standard). This caused pint ratings to drop — a "new" 50-pint unit is roughly equivalent to an "old" 70-pint unit. Always check for post-2019 labeling.

5 Tips to Cut Your Dehumidifier's Running Costs Even Further

  1. Set your humidistat to 45–50% RH, not lower. Every percentage point below 45% means more runtime and more electricity. 45–50% is the EPA sweet spot for health and efficiency.
  2. Use continuous drain mode. A full bucket causes the unit to auto-shut off. If it then takes hours for you to notice, humidity climbs back up — meaning the unit has to work harder to recover. A drain hose or built-in pump eliminates this.
