Slow Cooker vs Instant Pot vs Oven: Cheapest Way to Cook Dinner

The numbers on all three are pretty clear once you look at wattage and cook time together. Here's what it actually costs per meal, and which appliance to reach for first.

~$0.06Instant Pot cost per meal
~$0.20Slow cooker cost per meal (8 hrs)
~$0.40+Oven cost per meal (1.5 hrs)

The Instant Pot wins on electricity cost per meal, and it's not particularly close. A typical pressure-cooked dinner uses roughly 0.3–0.5 kWh start to finish. A slow cooker doing the same job over 8 hours uses about 1.2–1.6 kWh. A conventional electric oven running for 90 minutes uses 2–3 kWh. At a national average around $0.16 per kWh (EIA, 2026), those differences add up fast — especially if you're cooking dinner most nights of the week.

That said, none of these appliances live in isolation. The right one depends on what you're cooking, how much time you have, and whether you care about summer cooling costs on top of the cooking cost itself. We'll work through all of it.

How the Numbers Were Calculated

To keep this apples-to-apples, we used $0.16/kWh as the electricity rate — close to the U.S. average right now, though your rate may differ by several cents in either direction. If you're in California or New England, add 30–50% to the costs below. If you're in the South or Midwest, subtract 10–20%.

Wattage figures come from typical nameplate ratings: slow cookers generally run 200–300 watts on low, up to 300 watts on high; Instant Pots draw 700–1,200 watts at peak (during pressurization) but spend most of the cook time in a low-power "keep warm" phase, so average draw across a full cook is much lower; and a conventional electric oven pulls 2,400 watts when its element is actively on, cycling to maintain temperature, giving an effective average of around 1,500–2,000 watts over a bake cycle.

Appliance Typical wattage (active) Typical cook time Est. kWh per meal Cost per meal @ $0.16/kWh
Instant Pot (6 qt) 700–1,200 W peak; ~150 W average 30–45 min total 0.3–0.5 kWh ~$0.05–$0.08
Slow Cooker (6 qt, low) 200–300 W 6–8 hours 1.2–2.0 kWh ~$0.19–$0.32
Slow Cooker (6 qt, high) 250–300 W 3–4 hours 0.75–1.2 kWh ~$0.12–$0.19
Electric Oven 2,400 W (cycles to ~1,500 W avg) 60–90 min 1.5–3.0 kWh ~$0.24–$0.48

A few things worth noting in that table. First, the slow cooker on high for 3–4 hours is cheaper than the oven and gets reasonably close to the Instant Pot's cost. Second, the oven's range is wide — a 60-minute roast at 375°F costs a lot less than a 90-minute braise at 325°F with preheat. And third, these are per-meal estimates assuming you're using the whole appliance for one dish. If you're baking a single potato in a full-size oven, the cost-per-serving goes up considerably.

We've covered the microwave vs oven vs stove comparison separately if you want to see how those stack up too — the microwave story is similar to the Instant Pot in some ways.

Where the Instant Pot Wins (and Where It Doesn't)

The Instant Pot's efficiency advantage comes from pressure cooking's physics: higher pressure raises the boiling point of water, which means food cooks at a higher temperature in a sealed environment, dramatically cutting time. A chicken breast that takes 45 minutes in the oven takes 10–12 minutes under pressure. A pot of dried beans that would simmer for 90 minutes on the stove takes 25–30 minutes in a pressure cooker. Less time running means less electricity used, full stop.

Where it doesn't replace the oven: anything that needs dry heat, browning, or a crust. Roasted vegetables, baked bread, casseroles, pizzas — none of these work in a pressure cooker. An Instant Pot can sauté (using its built-in heating element), but the results aren't the same as oven roasting. So if your dinner involves a sheet pan of vegetables or a baked pasta dish, you're using the oven regardless.

The Instant Pot also requires more active setup than a slow cooker — you're sealing a lid, managing pressure release, and the cook time is more precise. That's fine for most people, but it's worth saying.

When the Slow Cooker Still Makes Sense

The slow cooker's appeal has never really been about electricity cost — it's about convenience. You set it in the morning and dinner is ready when you get home. That matters. And if you're comparing it to the oven for a long braise (pot roast, chili, pulled chicken), the slow cooker is significantly cheaper to run over those same hours. An oven running for 3–4 hours at 325°F costs $0.70–$1.00 or more. The slow cooker on high for the same duration costs $0.12–$0.19.

The slow cooker also wins on heat spillover. An oven running for hours dumps heat into your kitchen, and in summer, your air conditioner then has to remove it. A 1,500-watt-average oven running 90 minutes adds a meaningful thermal load to the room. A 250-watt slow cooker does the same, but at a fraction of the intensity. If you're running central AC or a window unit, this secondary cost is real — it's just harder to put an exact number on because it depends on your AC's efficiency and local rates. We've looked at similar dynamics in the air fryer vs oven comparison.

The Summer Cooling Penalty on Oven Use

This piece often gets skipped, but it's worth a quick look. When you run a full-size oven in summer, you're adding heat to a space your AC is already fighting to cool. The DOE estimates that cooking contributes meaningfully to household cooling loads. A rough estimate: if your AC runs at a SEER of 14 and you're paying $0.16/kWh, every extra kWh of heat added to the house costs you something in the range of $0.04–$0.08 in additional cooling. That's not dramatic per session, but across a summer's worth of daily dinners it adds a few dollars to the tally against the oven.

Slow cookers and Instant Pots vent very little heat into the room by comparison. This is one practical reason to use them in July even if you don't care deeply about the electricity math.

The Three Main Scenarios and What to Use

So the three main situations you're probably choosing between:

First — you want the cheapest possible dinner and you're okay with planning ahead. Use the Instant Pot. For soups, stews, beans, lentils, whole grains, and braised meats, it's the cheapest and fastest option. A full pot of chicken soup costs around $0.06 in electricity. That's about as low as countertop cooking gets.

Second — you want a hands-off dinner while you're at work or otherwise occupied all day. Use the slow cooker on low. You'll spend $0.19–$0.32 in electricity, which is still well under the oven and doesn't require you to be home. The convenience premium over the Instant Pot is real and reasonable.

Third — you're making something that genuinely needs the oven (baking, roasting, anything needing a crust). Use the oven, but try to be efficient about it: fill it when you can, avoid preheating longer than necessary, and consider whether a toaster oven or air fryer can handle the job instead for smaller portions. We've covered that air fryer vs oven question in detail if your portions are small enough to fit.

Worth asking: do you already own a slow cooker or Instant Pot that's sitting in a cabinet? If yes, shifting even 3 dinners a week away from the oven gets you $40–$60 back per year for essentially no effort.

FAQ

Does a slow cooker use more electricity than an Instant Pot?

Over a full cook, a slow cooker typically uses slightly more electricity than an Instant Pot — roughly 1.2–1.6 kWh on a long cycle vs. 0.3–0.5 kWh in a pressure cooker. But both are far cheaper than a conventional oven.

How much does it cost to run an oven for one hour?

A standard electric oven draws about 2,400 watts when the element is on, but it cycles, so average draw is closer to 1,500–2,000 watts. At $0.16/kWh, an hour of baking costs roughly $0.24–$0.32.

Is a slow cooker cheaper to run than an oven for a whole day?

Yes, significantly. A slow cooker running 8 hours uses around 1.2–1.6 kWh total. An oven running 8 hours would use 12–16 kWh. The slow cooker wins on a long cook by a wide margin.

Can I use my Instant Pot instead of an oven to save money?

For soups, stews, beans, grains, and braised meats, yes. The Instant Pot can replace the oven for most slow-cooked dishes and does it in far less time for a fraction of the electricity cost. For baking or roasting, the oven is still your only realistic option.

Does cooking method affect my home's cooling costs in summer?

It can. An oven running for an hour or more adds meaningful heat to your kitchen, which your AC then has to remove. Slow cookers and Instant Pots generate far less ambient heat, making them genuinely better choices in summer if you're running central or window AC.

In Short

Use the Instant Pot whenever the recipe allows — it's the cheapest option by a clear margin. Use the slow cooker when you need it running unattended all day; it's still well ahead of the oven on cost. Save the oven for things that actually need it: baking, roasting, browning. If you don't own a pressure cooker yet, a 6-quart Instant Pot pays for itself in about 12–18 months if you use it four nights a week, and it saves you real time on top of the electricity savings.
