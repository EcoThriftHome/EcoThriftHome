Spring Energy Checklist: Prepare Your Home for Cooling Season

Twelve things to do before it gets hot — covering your AC, air sealing, windows, appliances and thermostat so you're not scrambling when the first heat wave hits.

The best time to get your home ready for cooling season is a few weeks before you actually need the AC. That means mid-April to early May for most of the country — before HVAC appointment books fill up and before the first stretch of 90-degree days exposes every problem you didn't know you had.

This checklist works through the things that actually move the needle on your summer electricity bill: the mechanical (your AC and filters), the envelope (air leaks and windows), and the behavioral (thermostat settings, ceiling fans, heat-generating habits). None of this is complicated. Most of it costs under $30. A few items are free.

1. Test your AC before you need it

Run your central AC or window unit for 15 to 20 minutes on a mild spring day — 65°F outside is fine. You're checking three things: does cool air actually come out, is the airflow strong, and are there any unusual sounds (grinding, rattling, or a buzzing that wasn't there last year). If something's off, you want to find out in April with a two-day wait for a technician, not in July with a ten-day wait at emergency rates.

For central air, also walk outside and look at the condenser unit. The fins should be reasonably clear of debris. After a winter of leaves, cottonwood fluff and whatever else accumulated, they often aren't. A gentle rinse with a garden hose (spraying from the inside out if you can) handles most of it. Don't use a pressure washer — it bends the fins and reduces efficiency.

2. Replace the HVAC filter

A clogged filter is one of the cheapest problems to fix and one of the most consistently ignored. The DOE estimates a dirty filter can increase cooling energy use by 5 to 15 percent, because the system has to work harder to pull air through. A standard 1-inch filter runs $5 to $12. The savings math is straightforward: if your summer cooling bill averages $180 a month and a clean filter saves even 8 percent, that's $14 a month, or $42 over a three-month cooling season — from a $10 filter.

Put a fresh one in at the start of cooling season, then check it monthly. If you're running the system all day or have pets, it may need changing every 45 to 60 days rather than 90. We've got more detail on filter schedules in our post on HVAC filter replacement schedules.

3. Check and adjust ceiling fan direction

Ceiling fans need to spin counterclockwise in summer (viewed from below) to push air straight down and create a wind-chill effect. Most fans shipped in winter mode will be spinning clockwise; there's usually a small switch on the motor housing, or a setting in the app if it's a smart fan.

The practical upside is that this wind-chill effect lets most people raise the thermostat by about 4°F without feeling warmer. At 3 percent savings per degree, that's roughly a 12 percent reduction in your cooling costs — from flipping a switch. One note: fans cool people, not rooms. Turn them off when you leave a room, or you're just burning electricity for no one. We've covered the full math on this in ceiling fan direction for summer and winter.

4. Set (or program) your thermostat for summer

The DOE's guidance is 78°F when you're home and awake, and 85°F (or off) when you're away or asleep. Every degree below 78°F adds about 3 percent to your cooling costs. That's not a huge number per degree, but a household running at 72°F instead of 78°F is paying roughly 18 percent more to cool the same space.

If you have a programmable or smart thermostat, now is a good time to update the schedule from your winter heating program to a summer cooling one. The setback when you're away is where most of the savings happen — there's no point keeping the house at 78°F all day when nobody's home until 5 PM. If you're shopping for a thermostat, our roundup of smart thermostats under $100 covers solid options that pay for themselves in a season or two.

5. Air-seal before the heat arrives

Air leaks are a year-round problem, but they're especially costly in summer because every gap lets hot humid air in while your conditioned air escapes. The highest-value spots to check in spring are window frames, door frames, the sill plate where your walls meet the foundation, and any penetrations where pipes or wires enter the house.

A simple candle or incense test on a breezy day will find most significant leaks — the flame or smoke deflects toward any gap. Caulk handles stationary joints between dissimilar materials; weatherstripping handles moving parts like door edges. Neither is expensive. A tube of paintable caulk is about $5, and a full door weatherstripping kit runs $12 to $20. The payback on sealing a drafty door or a leaky window frame is typically under a year.

If you haven't thought much about air sealing below your living space, the post on air sealing crawl spaces and basements explains where the biggest leaks usually hide and what to do about them.

6. Deal with window heat gain

Windows are where a significant amount of summer heat enters. South- and west-facing windows are the main culprits on a hot afternoon. The options vary by budget and how permanent you want the solution to be:

  • Thermal or blackout curtains ($25–$60 per panel) block radiant heat when closed. They work best if you close them before the sun hits those windows, not after the room is already warm.
  • Window film ($15–$40 for a window kit) is a more permanent option that cuts solar heat gain without darkening the room as much as curtains. Good for windows where you want natural light. We've covered this in detail in window film and solar shades.
  • Exterior shading (awnings, shutters, shade trees) is the most effective option if you have it, because it blocks heat before it reaches the glass. If you're planting shade trees, place them on the south or west side where they'll do the most work.

Worth asking before you buy anything: which windows actually heat up your living space in the afternoon? Start there rather than treating every window the same.

7. Inspect and clear your window AC units (if applicable)

Window AC units that sat in storage over winter should be checked before you install them. Look at the filter — most slide out and can be rinsed with warm water and left to dry. Check the coils for dust and debris. Make sure the drain hole on the back or bottom isn't blocked; standing water inside the unit is both a mold risk and an efficiency problem.

Also check the seal around the unit where it sits in the window frame. That foam weatherstripping degrades over time and gaps let outside air pour in around the edges. Replacement foam seal kits are about $8 to $12 and take 15 minutes to swap out. For efficiency rankings on specific models, our window AC unit reviews break down the numbers by EER rating and room size.

8. Clear bathroom and kitchen exhaust fan paths

Exhaust fans pull hot, humid air out of your home during cooking and showering — which is especially valuable in summer when you're also running AC. If the duct or the exterior vent cap is clogged with lint or debris, the fan isn't actually moving air anywhere. Take two minutes to check the exterior damper flap; it should open freely when the fan runs and close tight when it doesn't (to prevent hot outdoor air from coming back in).

Running an exhaust fan during and after cooking also reduces the heat load on your AC. A range hood or even a bathroom fan pointed toward the kitchen while cooking can remove enough heat and moisture to make a noticeable difference in a small home.

9. Shift heat-generating tasks to cooler hours

Your oven, dishwasher and dryer all add heat and humidity to your home. In summer, running them in the morning or in the evening after the outdoor temperature drops means your AC doesn't have to remove that heat during the hottest part of the day.

This costs nothing to change and reduces peak cooling load, which is when your AC works hardest. If your utility has time-of-use rates, running appliances off-peak also cuts what you pay per kilowatt-hour. It's one of those adjustments that sounds small but compounds over three to four months of hot weather.

Using an air fryer or toaster oven instead of a full-size oven in summer is a real upgrade here — they add less heat to the room and use considerably less energy. We compared the numbers in air fryer vs. oven energy use.

10. Check your attic insulation and ventilation

Attic heat is a major driver of cooling costs. On a 90°F day, an unventilated attic can reach 150°F or more, and that heat radiates down through your ceiling. Two things help here: adequate insulation (at least R-38 for most climates, R-49 to R-60 in hot climates) and proper attic ventilation to move hot air out.

Poke your head into the attic in spring and check two things: the depth of your insulation (you can find R-value estimates by measuring how deep the insulation sits and looking up the material) and whether the ridge vent and soffit vents are clear and unobstructed. Blocked soffit vents are very common, especially in homes with blown-in insulation that has migrated toward the eaves. Foam baffles ($1 to $2 each) keep the insulation back and the air channel open.

If your attic insulation is thin or non-existent, it's worth looking at seriously — the DOE estimates attic insulation can cut heating and cooling costs by up to 20 percent. We've written about this in attic insulation and what it can actually save.

11. Check dehumidifier readiness (humid climates)

In humid climates, moisture is often as much of a comfort and cost problem as heat. High humidity makes 78°F feel noticeably worse than 78°F at low humidity, which pushes people to drop the thermostat further. A dehumidifier in a consistently humid basement or ground floor can let you raise the AC setpoint by a degree or two without feeling it.

Before summer, check that your dehumidifier's coils and filter are clean, that the bucket drains properly (or that your continuous drain line is clear), and that the humidistat is set where you want it (45 to 50% relative humidity is a good target). We've got a full post on dehumidifiers vs. AC and when each makes sense.

12. Look at your electricity rate and any summer rate changes

Some utilities switch to time-of-use pricing in summer, or raise rates during peak hours (typically 2 PM to 7 PM). If yours does, the highest-impact change is shifting your AC setback schedule to precool the house before 2 PM and then let the temperature rise a few degrees during peak hours. The house's thermal mass helps hold the temperature; your AC doesn't have to run as hard; and you're not paying peak rates while doing it.

Call your utility or log into your account to check whether your rate structure changes seasonally. It takes five minutes and can easily be worth $20 to $40 over a hot summer if you adjust your thermostat schedule accordingly.

Frequently asked questions

When should I start my spring cooling season prep?

Aim to have everything done before the first stretch of days above 80°F in your area. That's usually mid-April to late May depending on your climate. The key is finishing before HVAC companies book out and before you need the AC to actually run.

Should I turn my AC on before it gets hot to test it?

Yes. Run it for 15 to 20 minutes on a mild day in April or early May. If it's not cooling, making unusual noises or the airflow seems weak, you have time to get a technician out before the summer rush when appointment waits stretch to two or three weeks.

How often do I need to change the HVAC filter during cooling season?

Every 60 to 90 days for a standard 1-inch filter in most homes. If you have pets, allergies or the system is running all day, check it monthly. A clogged filter can increase your cooling energy use by 5 to 15 percent, according to the DOE.

Does my ceiling fan direction matter in summer?

Yes. Set fans to spin counterclockwise (when viewed from below) in summer so they push air straight down. This creates a wind-chill effect that lets most people raise the thermostat 4°F without noticing a difference — that's roughly 10 percent off your cooling bill.

Is it worth having my AC professionally serviced every year?

For central AC systems, a tune-up every two to three years is reasonable for most homes, unless it's older or showing performance issues. Annual service makes more sense for systems over 10 years old or if your bills have been climbing unexpectedly. What matters every year regardless: clean or replace the filter, clear debris from the outdoor unit and test it before the heat arrives.

What's the best thermostat setting for summer to save money?

The DOE suggests 78°F when you're home and awake, and 85°F or off when you're away or asleep. Each degree lower than 78°F adds roughly 3 percent to your cooling costs. A programmable or smart thermostat handles the schedule automatically.

In short

Test the AC now, replace the filter, flip your ceiling fans, update your thermostat schedule, and seal any obvious gaps around windows and doors. Those five things, done before the first real heat wave, will cover most of what you need. The rest of the checklist — attic checks, window film, dehumidifier maintenance — matters more for some homes than others, depending on your climate and how your house is built. Start with the basics; they pay off in every home.
