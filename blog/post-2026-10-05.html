There is an embarrassingly cheap fix hiding at the bottom of most front doors in America. A gap as thin as a pencil width — roughly 1/8 inch — lets in as much cold air as a 2.4-inch-diameter hole in your wall, according to data from the Oak Ridge National Laboratory. Yet most homeowners walk past that gap every single day and crank the thermostat instead.

Door draft stoppers are not glamorous. But they are one of the fastest-payback energy upgrades available, often returning their cost in a single heating season. The challenge is that the market is flooded with products ranging from genuinely effective to nearly useless. This guide ranks the main categories and specific products by two things that actually matter: how well they seal air and how much they cost.

Key Takeaway: Permanently installed door sweeps and automatic door bottoms seal up to 90% more air than fabric draft snakes, but even a quality $12 fabric stopper on an interior door can meaningfully reduce heat transfer between rooms.

Why Door Gaps Cost You More Than You Think

Before we rank products, it helps to understand what you're actually losing. The U.S. Department of Energy consistently points to air infiltration as one of the largest single sources of residential energy waste.

"Sealing air leaks and adding insulation are the most cost-effective ways to improve energy efficiency and comfort. Drafts around doors and windows can account for 25 to 30 percent of a home's heating and cooling energy use."

— U.S. Department of Energy, Energy Saver: Air Sealing Your Home

For a home spending $1,500 per year on heating and cooling, that's up to $450 disappearing through gaps — and your front door is one of the biggest individual contributors. A standard exterior door with no bottom seal can have an effective gap of 1/4 inch or more, especially in older homes where the door has settled or the flooring has warped.

The 4 Types of Door Draft Stoppers, Ranked by Effectiveness

Not all draft stoppers work the same way. Before picking a product, understand which category fits your situation. We've ranked them from most to least effective at actual air sealing.

1. Automatic Door Bottoms (Most Effective)

An automatic door bottom is a mechanism installed into the bottom edge of the door. When the door closes, a pin or cam triggers a rubber or neoprene seal to drop down and make firm contact with the threshold. When the door opens, the seal retracts so it doesn't drag across flooring.

This is the gold standard for exterior doors. Because the seal is mechanically compressed against the threshold every single time the door closes, you get consistent, repeatable contact with no user error. Real-world testing shows these can reduce door-bottom airflow by 85–95% compared to an unsealed door.

The downside: installation takes about 30–45 minutes, requires drilling, and these units cost $40–$120. They're worth every cent on a frequently used exterior door in a cold climate.

2. Door Sweeps (Very Effective)

A door sweep is a strip of metal or rigid plastic with a rubber, vinyl, or brush seal that screws to the bottom of the door. Unlike automatic door bottoms, sweeps maintain contact with the floor at all times — which means they can wear down carpet or drag on rugs, but also means they never "forget" to seal.

Quality door sweeps with a flexible rubber fin can reduce bottom-gap air leakage by 70–85%. They cost $10–$35 and installation takes 15 minutes with a screwdriver. This is our top recommendation for most exterior doors where budget is a concern.

3. Threshold Seals / Door Shoes (Effective with Proper Fit)

A door shoe wraps around the bottom of the door and includes a vinyl or rubber bulb that compresses against the threshold when the door closes. These provide excellent seals — comparable to automatic door bottoms — but require removing the door to install. They're best for new door installations or when you're already refinishing a door.

4. Fabric Draft Stoppers / Door Snakes (Moderate, Portable)

The classic fabric tube filled with sand, foam, or recycled material gets placed at the base of the door manually. These do not seal air in the technical sense — they create a soft barrier that slows convective airflow but doesn't stop it the way a compressed rubber seal does.

Independent testing has found that fabric stoppers reduce airflow through a door gap by roughly 30–50% depending on gap size and fabric weight. That's meaningful for interior doors between a conditioned room and an unconditioned garage, but it's not a substitute for a proper exterior door sweep. Their advantages: zero installation, zero tools, move them wherever you need them.

How to Choose the Right Type for Your Situation

Use this quick decision framework before you buy:

  • Exterior door, cold climate, used daily: Automatic door bottom (Pemko 315CN). The consistent mechanical seal justifies the installation time and cost.
  • Exterior door, moderate climate, or budget-first: Quality door sweep (M-D Building Products or Xcluder). Fifteen minutes with a screwdriver and you've sealed the gap for years.
  • Interior door between conditioned and unconditioned space: Double-sided fabric stopper (Holikme twin pack). No tools, no damage, and you can move it seasonally.
  • Renting and can't modify the door: Fabric stopper is your only hardware-free option. It won't perform like a sweep, but it's far better than nothing.
  • New door installation or full door refurbishment: Door shoe or door bottom as part of the new hardware package — you're already off the hinges, so do it right.

Don't Forget the Sides and Top

A draft stopper only addresses the door bottom. If you can feel a draft at the sides or top of your door frame, you'll also need to replace or add door frame weatherstripping. V-strip (tension seal) weatherstripping is the most durable option for door sides — it compresses when the door closes and springs back without losing its shape for years. Foam tape is cheaper but flattens out within one season under compression. We'll cover weatherstripping in a dedicated post, but don't leave the sides unsealed after fixing the bottom or you're leaving money on the table.

The Bottom Line

Door draft stoppers are one of the highest-ROI weatherization upgrades you can make. The best product for most homeowners is a quality door sweep in the $15–$30 range — it installs in under 30 minutes, seals 70–85% of bottom-gap air leakage, and pays for itself many times over in a single heating season. If you have a heavily used front door in a cold climate and the budget for it, step up to an automatic door bottom for near-complete sealing that lasts years with zero user effort. Save the fabric stoppers for interior doors and rental situations where hardware installation isn't possible.

Either way, start this weekend. Heating season is here, and a gap at the bottom of your door is burning money every hour the furnace runs.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much energy can a door draft stopper actually save?

The U.S. Department of Energy estimates that air leaks around doors and windows account for 25–30% of a home's heating and cooling energy use. A properly installed door draft stopper can meaningfully reduce that loss, saving $20–$80 per year per exterior door depending on your climate and energy rates.

What is the difference between a door sweep and a draft stopper?

A door sweep is a hardware strip permanently attached to the bottom edge of the door that seals the gap when the door closes. A draft stopper (or door snake) is a removable fabric tube placed at the base of the door. Sweeps generally outperform fabric stoppers in airtightness but require installation.

Do draft stoppers work on both sides of the door?

Double-sided draft stoppers with a fabric sleeve that extends under the door to both the interior and exterior sides provide the best seal for interior doors. For exterior doors, a permanently installed door sweep or automatic door bottom is more effective because it seals the gap mechanically.

How do I know if my door needs a draft stopper?

The incense stick test works well: on a windy day, hold a lit incense stick near the bottom of the door. If the smoke wavers or is pulled sideways, air is moving through the gap.
