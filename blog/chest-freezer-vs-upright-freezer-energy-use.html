Chest Freezer vs Upright Freezer: Which Uses Less Electricity?

Chest freezers win on energy use almost every time. Here's by how much, what drives the difference, and when it's reasonable to pick the upright anyway.

~$60typical annual cost, 15 cu ft upright

Chest freezers use less electricity than upright freezers of comparable size, usually 10–25% less per year. That gap comes down to basic physics and one feature most uprights include that most chest freezers don't. If your only goal is minimizing running costs, a chest freezer is the right call. But there are real reasons people choose uprights, and the energy penalty isn't enormous if you pick an efficient model.

Here's what actually drives the difference, what real numbers look like across common sizes, and how to make a smart choice for your situation.

Why Chest Freezers Use Less Energy

Three things work in a chest freezer's favor, and they all relate to how cold air behaves.

First — cold air sinks. When you open a chest freezer, the cold air stays inside because it has nowhere to go but down. With an upright, every time you open the door, the cold air tumbles straight out onto the floor. The compressor then has to bring the interior back down to temperature. If you're opening your freezer multiple times a day, this adds up.

Second — insulation geometry. A chest freezer's lid is thick and well-insulated, and the box shape means a relatively low surface-area-to-volume ratio. Uprights typically have more overall surface area for the same usable volume, which means slightly more heat leaking in from the room.

Third — frost-free vs. manual defrost. This is probably the biggest single driver. Most upright freezers sold today are frost-free, meaning they have a built-in heating element that cycles on periodically to melt frost off the evaporator coils. That heating element adds roughly 10–15% to annual energy use, and then the compressor has to remove that added heat. Most chest freezers are manual-defrost, so there's no heater cycling in the background. You defrost once or twice a year by hand; the freezer runs leaner the rest of the time.

The Location Factor Nobody Mentions

The numbers above assume the freezer sits in a conditioned space at around 70°F. ENERGY STAR tests are done under those conditions. If your freezer lives in an attached garage, the actual consumption can be 10–25% higher in summer months, because the compressor is working against 85–95°F ambient air instead of 70°F. This applies equally to both types, but it's worth factoring in if you're trying to predict real costs.

A basement or utility room at 60–65°F is actually better than the 70°F test condition, so freezers in cool basements sometimes beat their rated figures. Worth knowing when you're deciding where to put the thing.

How Old Is Your Current Freezer?

If you're deciding whether to replace an existing unit rather than choosing between two new ones, the age question matters a lot. Freezer efficiency improved substantially between 2010 and 2020. An upright freezer from 2008–2012 might be using 500–600 kWh per year where a current ENERGY STAR chest of similar size uses 180–220 kWh. At $0.17/kWh, that's a gap of $50–$70 per year. A new ENERGY STAR chest freezer at $200–$300 could pay for itself in 3–5 years purely on electricity savings, before accounting for any rebates.

A plug-in energy monitor is the fastest way to know what your current freezer actually costs. Plug it in, let it run for a week, multiply by 52. If the number surprises you, the upgrade math is easy.

When an Upright Makes Sense Anyway

The energy advantage of a chest freezer is real, but it doesn't necessarily win the whole decision. A few situations where the upright is a reasonable choice:

You need to find things quickly and regularly. Chest freezers are notorious for becoming black holes. Things at the bottom get buried, forgotten, and eventually thrown out. If wasted food is a problem in your house, the organizational advantage of an upright with shelves and door bins may save you more in food waste than you spend in extra electricity. This is a genuine trade-off worth thinking about honestly.

Floor space is tight and vertical space is available. A 15 cu ft upright has a footprint of roughly 24–28 inches wide by 24–28 inches deep. A chest freezer with equivalent volume might be 60+ inches wide. If you're fitting into a narrow utility closet or along a wall, the upright works where the chest won't.

You have mobility or accessibility constraints. Reaching to the bottom of a chest freezer isn't trivial. For anyone with back or shoulder limitations, an upright with eye-level shelves is functionally far better.

None of these situations make the energy math disappear, but $15–$25 per year is a modest penalty for a genuinely better fit in your home.

Picking an Efficient Model of Either Type

Whatever you choose, ENERGY STAR certification is the starting filter. ENERGY STAR freezers must use at least 10% less energy than the federal minimum standard, and the best-performing models beat that by a wider margin. You can look up specific models on the ENERGY STAR product finder to compare estimated kWh, which is more reliable than comparing wattage specs from product listings.

For chest freezers, the most efficient models in the 7 cu ft range from brands like Midea and GE typically land around 150–170 kWh per year. For uprights, you'll generally find the most efficient frost-free models from the same brands in the 300–330 kWh range for 14–15 cu ft, with manual-defrost uprights (harder to find, but they exist) closer to 230–260 kWh.

One more thing worth checking: some utility companies offer rebates on ENERGY STAR freezers, usually $25–$75. It's a five-minute search on your utility's website and occasionally makes the upgrade math considerably faster. See what rebates and credits are currently available before you buy.

Running Two Freezers vs One Larger One

Some households end up with two smaller freezers because one filled up. If that's you, it's worth knowing that one larger-capacity freezer almost always uses less electricity than two smaller ones combined, even when you're accounting for the full volume. Two separate compressors, two shells losing heat to the room, two defrost cycles if both are frost-free. If you're in that situation, consolidating into one larger chest freezer is likely the more efficient path, and probably cheaper to buy outright too.

Keeping Either Freezer Running Efficiently

A few maintenance habits apply regardless of which type you have. The condenser coils on some models (usually on the back or underneath) can accumulate dust and cause the compressor to work harder. Cleaning them once a year, the same way you'd clean refrigerator coils, can make a small but real difference. Check the door gaskets periodically too — a gasket that no longer seals cleanly lets warm air in constantly. Run a dollar bill in the closed door; if it slides out easily, the seal has gone soft and replacing the gasket is worth doing.

Keep the freezer at least two-thirds full if you can. A full freezer holds temperature better when the door opens because the mass of frozen food acts as thermal ballast. If yours tends to run half-empty, fill the gaps with water-filled containers or gallon jugs. This matters more for uprights than chest freezers, but it helps either way.

FAQ

How much does it cost to run a chest freezer per year?

A typical 7-cubic-foot chest freezer uses roughly 150–200 kWh per year. At $0.17/kWh, that works out to about $26–$34 per year.

How much does it cost to run an upright freezer per year?

A comparable 15-cubic-foot upright freezer uses roughly 300–400 kWh per year. At $0.17/kWh, that's about $51–$68 per year. Frost-free models run higher, sometimes 10–15% more than manual-defrost uprights.

Does a frost-free freezer use more electricity?

Yes. The automatic defrost heating element cycles on periodically and adds roughly 10–15% to annual energy use compared to a manual-defrost model of the same size. Most uprights sold today are frost-free; most chest freezers are manual-defrost.

Does where you put your freezer affect how much electricity it uses?

Significantly. A freezer in a hot garage in summer can use 10–25% more electricity than the same unit in a conditioned basement or utility room, because the compressor has to work harder against higher ambient temperatures. ENERGY STAR ratings are based on 70°F ambient conditions.

Is it worth replacing an old freezer with a new ENERGY STAR model?

Often yes, if the old unit is 10+ years old. Freezers from the early 2010s or older can use 300–500 kWh per year more than a current ENERGY STAR chest freezer. At $0.17/kWh, that gap can exceed $50–$85 per year, so a new unit paying for itself in 3–5 years is realistic.

Should I keep my freezer full to save energy?

Mostly yes. A full freezer holds temperature better when the door opens, so the compressor runs less. If yours is often half-empty, filling the space with water jugs works fine. This matters more for uprights, where cold air spills out every time you open the door.

The Short Version

A chest freezer will cost you roughly $26–$41 per year to run depending on size. A frost-free upright of similar capacity runs $51–$68. The gap is real but not enormous — maybe $15–$25 annually. If you can live with the organization trade-off, a manual-defrost chest freezer with an ENERGY STAR rating is the cheapest option to run. If you go with an upright, look for a manual-defrost model or at least verify the ENERGY STAR kWh estimate, and put it in the coolest room you have. And if your current freezer is more than ten years old, plug a monitor into it before assuming the upgrade isn't worth it.
