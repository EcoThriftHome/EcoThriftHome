Dryer Balls vs Dryer Sheets: Do Wool Balls Actually Cut Drying Time?

Wool dryer balls do work — just not dramatically. Here's the real math on time savings, cost savings, and what you'd actually give up by ditching dryer sheets.

Wool dryer balls do reduce drying time — but the reduction is real without being dramatic. Most tests and manufacturer data put it at roughly 10 to 25 percent per load, depending on load size, fabric type, and how many balls you use. That translates to a few minutes on a light load and maybe 10 to 15 minutes on a heavy one. Worth knowing about, but not a revolution in your laundry routine.

Whether that makes them worth switching to depends on what you're comparing against, so let's work through the actual numbers before making any recommendation.

How dryer balls reduce drying time

The mechanism is simple. Clothes in a dryer naturally clump together as they tumble, especially towels and heavy cotton. When fabric clumps, the hot air circulates around the outside of the bundle instead of through it, and drying slows down. Dryer balls physically separate the clothes as they tumble, creating more air pockets throughout the load. More airflow means more evaporation, and the cycle ends sooner.

Wool also absorbs a small amount of moisture at the start of the cycle and releases it as heat, which some proponents say helps dry clothes faster. That effect is probably minor compared to the mechanical separation benefit, but it doesn't hurt.

Dryer sheets, for comparison, do nothing for drying time. They're a one-use strip of fabric softener that coats your clothes and the dryer drum with a waxy lubricant. Soft clothes, reduced static, a scent — but zero effect on how long the cycle runs.

The actual cost comparison

To put real numbers on this, we need a few assumptions. A typical electric dryer draws about 5,000 watts (5 kW). At the U.S. average electricity rate of around $0.17 per kWh, running the dryer costs roughly $0.85 per hour. The average household does about 5 loads per week, and a full cycle runs somewhere between 45 and 60 minutes. Call it 50 minutes per load.

So if you're on the optimistic end of the range (25% reduction), you might save around $48 a year on electricity. At the conservative end (10%), it's closer to $18. Your actual dryer and your local electricity rate will determine where you land. If you're paying $0.25/kWh — common in many states now — those numbers go up by about 47%.

Then add in the dryer sheet cost you'd stop buying. A box of 200 Bounce dryer sheets runs about $12 to $15, and at one sheet per load for 260 loads a year, that's roughly $16 to $20 annually. A six-ball set of wool dryer balls costs $10 to $15 and lasts for 1,000+ loads (more on that in the FAQ below). So you're eliminating an ongoing cost and replacing it with a one-time purchase that pays for itself in a few months.

The combined benefit — electricity savings plus dryer sheet savings — runs roughly $35 to $65 per year depending on your dryer, your rate, and how fully the balls reduce cycle time. That's not dramatic, but a $12 purchase that saves $35+ a year and never needs replacing for four years is a reasonable trade.

What you give up by ditching dryer sheets

This is where we should be honest rather than cheerleading. Dryer sheets do a few things that wool balls don't replicate as well.

Static cling is the main one. Dryer sheets are genuinely effective at neutralizing static because they deposit a conductive coating on fabric. Wool balls reduce static somewhat by keeping clothes separated — which limits friction — but they don't eliminate it the way sheets do, especially with synthetic fabrics like polyester. If your household wears a lot of synthetics, or you live in a very dry climate, you may notice more static with wool balls.

Scent is the other obvious difference. Dryer sheets leave a consistent fragrance. Wool balls don't have any scent unless you add essential oils (which does work, with some limitations — see the FAQ). If your household is attached to "fresh laundry" smell, that's worth factoring in.

Fabric softening is roughly comparable, though dryer sheets have a slight edge on heavy cotton like towels. Neither product adds the kind of deep softness you'd get from liquid fabric softener in the wash.

Plastic dryer balls vs. wool dryer balls

You'll also see blue plastic dryer balls in stores, usually priced around $5 to $8 for a pair. These work on the same separation principle and they're durable — they don't wear out the way wool does. The tradeoff is that they can be louder in the drum, they don't absorb moisture the way wool does, and there are some concerns about plastic off-gassing at high heat over time, though no definitive research we've seen on household dryers specifically. Wool is the more popular choice for those reasons, and the price difference is small enough that we'd generally go with wool.

So what should you actually do?

So the three main reasons to switch from dryer sheets to wool balls are: First — you eliminate an ongoing purchase cost entirely. Second — you get a genuine (if modest) reduction in drying time and electricity use. Third — there's no chemical coating deposited on your clothes or the drum, which matters to some households, especially those with sensitive skin.

The case for keeping dryer sheets is simpler: they're better at static control, the scent is more reliable, and you're already buying them without thinking about it. For most households, the dryer sheet habit is a small, manageable cost that works. Switching to wool balls requires a one-time intentional purchase and a small adjustment in expectations.

One thing worth checking before you decide: how long are your cycles actually running? If your dryer takes 70 to 80 minutes for a normal load, there may be a bigger issue — a clogged lint trap or a partially blocked vent duct — that's the real problem. Fixing that will save far more than dryer balls can. See our post on how much it costs to run a dryer for a fuller breakdown of what's driving your dryer's energy use.

If your cycles are already reasonable and you're mainly interested in the incremental savings plus eliminating the sheet-buying habit, wool balls are a straightforward call. Use six for large loads and towels, three for lighter loads. Don't bother with just one or two — you won't see meaningful separation.

If static is a persistent problem in your home, you might keep a small box of dryer sheets around for synthetic-heavy loads and use wool balls the rest of the time. That's a reasonable middle ground that still cuts your sheet consumption significantly.

Also worth noting: if you're already focused on laundry energy use, the bigger savings are in cold-water washing. We cover that in detail separately — see our post on how cold water laundry saves up to 90% on washing energy. And if you want to understand your dryer's draw more precisely, a plug-in power monitor on the dryer outlet will show you the actual wattage and let you calculate exactly what each load is costing.

FAQ

How many wool dryer balls should I use per load?

Three balls for a small or medium load, six for a large or heavy load like towels and bedding. Fewer balls means less separation and less benefit.

Do dryer balls work with a gas dryer?

Yes. The mechanism is the same — tumbling separates clothes so hot air circulates better. Gas dryers run hotter than electric, so drying times are already shorter, but the balls still help.

Do wool dryer balls reduce static cling?

Somewhat. They work better when you avoid over-drying, which is a main cause of static. Adding a small safety pin to one ball or a crumpled piece of aluminum foil helps further. They're not as effective as dryer sheets on static for synthetic fabrics.

Can I add scent to wool dryer balls?

Yes. Put a few drops of essential oil on each ball and let them dry for 15 minutes before tossing them in. The scent fades faster than a dryer sheet, but it's there.

How long do wool dryer balls last?

Most sets are rated for 1,000 loads or more. At five loads a week, that's roughly four years per set. The wool can pill and compress over time; when they noticeably shrink or harden, it's time to replace them.

In short

Buy a six-pack of wool dryer balls if you want to eliminate the dryer sheet habit and pick up a small reduction in drying time and electricity use in the process. Expect to save roughly $35 to $65 a year combined, depending on your dryer and electricity rate. Don't expect them to solve a serious static problem if you're drying a lot of synthetics — keep a few dryer sheets around for those loads if needed.
