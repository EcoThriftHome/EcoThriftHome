Smart Thermostat Geofencing: Save Money Every Time You Leave Home

Your thermostat doesn't need to know the clock—it needs to know where you are. Here's how geofencing works, what it actually saves, and when to use it instead of a schedule.

Geofencing on a smart thermostat uses your phone's location to automatically set back the temperature when you leave home and restore it before you return. That's the whole thing. No schedule to maintain, no manual adjustments, no coming home to a cold house—or a house that's been heating empty rooms all day.

Whether it's worth turning on (and how much it actually saves) depends on your home, your HVAC system, and how predictable your day is. We'll work through all of it.

How Geofencing Works on a Smart Thermostat

You set a virtual boundary—a radius around your home address—inside the thermostat's app. When your phone crosses outside that boundary, the thermostat switches into an "away" mode: it relaxes the temperature target, so the system runs less. When you cross back inside the boundary on your way home, it starts bringing the house back to your comfort setpoint, ideally timed so the house feels right by the time you walk in the door.

The radius is adjustable. Set it too tight (a few hundred feet) and the thermostat might flip away when you're just taking the trash out. Set it too wide and you're giving up 20 minutes of head start on a cold morning. Most people land somewhere between half a mile and two miles and leave it there.

The thermostat checks your phone's location using background GPS services—the same technology your navigation app uses when it's running in the background. It's low-power, but it does require the thermostat app to have location permissions set to "always" (not just "while using"). Worth double-checking if geofencing doesn't seem to be triggering.

The Savings Case: What the Numbers Actually Show

The U.S. Department of Energy has long said that turning your thermostat back 7–10°F for 8 hours a day can save roughly 10% on your annual heating and cooling bills. Geofencing automates exactly that setback, applied specifically to the hours you're away from home rather than a fixed schedule.

What that translates to in dollars depends on your bill. If you spend $200 a month on heating and cooling combined, a 10% reduction is $20 a month—or about $240 a year. If your bill is closer to $100 a month for HVAC, you're looking at closer to $100 annually. ENERGY STAR estimates that smart thermostat users who make good use of setbacks save around $50 per year on average, though that figure includes households who barely change their settings at all. Active use of features like geofencing pushes the number higher.

To put a rough number on your own situation: assume about $0.14 per kWh (close to the national average as of late 2026), a central air system that draws about 3,500 watts when running, and that geofencing cuts runtime by 2 hours a day on days you're out. That's 7 kWh saved per away-day, or about $1 per day. If you're out of the house 220 days a year (weekdays plus occasional weekends), that's around $220 annually. That's a reasonable ceiling for a detached house in a moderate climate with a central system. Your actual number will be lower or higher depending on climate, home size, and insulation.

Geofencing vs. a Fixed Schedule

A fixed schedule works well if your life is predictable: out the door at 7:30, back by 5:30, every weekday. If that describes you, a schedule and geofencing will produce similar results, and the schedule arguably does it more reliably since it doesn't depend on your phone having a signal.

Geofencing earns its keep when your schedule varies. If you work from home two or three days a week, leave at unpredictable times, travel for stretches, or have kids with inconsistent school schedules, a fixed schedule either leaves the house heating while someone's home or misses setback windows entirely. Geofencing adapts without you having to touch anything.

The other advantage of geofencing is weekends and holidays. Most people set up weekday and weekend schedules and forget that a long weekend trip, a vacation, or just a Saturday out means the thermostat is running a comfort schedule in an empty house. Geofencing catches those automatically.

Some thermostats let you run both simultaneously—a schedule as the baseline with geofencing as an override. That can work, but it's worth checking your thermostat's documentation to confirm how the two interact. If they conflict (the schedule says comfort, geofencing says away), the thermostat may default to one or the other in ways that aren't obvious.

Setting It Up: The Practical Steps

Setup is roughly the same across brands. After installing the thermostat and connecting it to your Wi-Fi, you open the app and look for a section called something like "Home/Away," "Geofencing," or "Smart Location." From there:

First, make sure the app has location permission set to "always" on your phone, not just "while using." Without this, the geofence trigger won't fire when the app is closed. On iOS this is under Settings > Privacy > Location Services > [thermostat app]. On Android, it's similar under App Permissions.

Second, set your away temperature. For heating, dropping 7–10°F from your comfort setpoint is the range where you get real savings without risking frozen pipes (in very cold climates, don't go below 55°F). For cooling, raising the setpoint 7–10°F from comfort works the same way. If your comfort temperature in winter is 70°F, an away setpoint of 62°F is reasonable.

Third, set the geofence radius. Start with one mile and adjust from there. If you find it triggers when you're just running to a nearby store for 15 minutes, you can tighten it or look for a "minimum away time" setting—some thermostats won't switch to away mode unless you've been outside the boundary for at least 5 or 10 minutes.

Fourth, if other people live in your household, have them install the same thermostat app and add their phones to the geofence. The thermostat should stay in comfort mode as long as any registered phone is inside the boundary.

Where Geofencing Doesn't Help Much

A few situations where it's less useful, honestly:

If you work from home full-time, there's rarely a window where the house is empty long enough to justify a setback and recovery cycle. The savings shrink considerably if you're only away for short errands.

If you have pets that are sensitive to temperature swings, your away setpoints are constrained regardless. A dog that can't tolerate dropping below 65°F limits how much you can set back in winter. The savings are still real, just smaller.

Heat pumps deserve a note here: they're less efficient at recovering quickly from large temperature setbacks than gas furnaces are. If you have a heat pump, consider a more modest setback (4–6°F instead of 10°F) to avoid the system working hard to recover. A Few Things to Check Before Assuming It's Working

Geofencing is one of those features that's easy to enable and forget, then wonder later why your bill didn't change much. A couple of things to verify:

Check the thermostat's history log (most apps have one) after a few days. It should show away events that correspond to times you were actually out. If it's not logging any away events, the location permission is probably the culprit.

Also check whether the app defaulted to running geofencing alongside a schedule. If your schedule has the house in comfort mode from 4pm to 10pm, geofencing won't be able to do anything useful during those hours even if you're out. It only helps if the thermostat would otherwise be holding a warmer or cooler setpoint while nobody's home.

Worth asking yourself: is the house genuinely empty for 4+ hours on most weekdays? If so, you're leaving savings on the table every day without it.

FAQ

How far away do you have to be before geofencing kicks in?

Most thermostats let you set the radius yourself, usually somewhere between half a mile and a few miles. The default on most devices is around half a mile to one mile. A tighter radius means faster setback but also more false triggers if you circle the block or stop near home.

Does geofencing drain my phone battery?

It uses a small amount of battery because the app checks your GPS position periodically. In practice, the impact is minor—most users report little to no noticeable difference. The thermostat app doesn't need to be running in the foreground; it uses low-power background location services.

What if I have multiple people in the house?

Most smart thermostats support multiple phones in the geofence. The thermostat typically stays in comfort mode until the last person leaves, and switches back when the first person returns. You set up each household member's phone in the thermostat app.

Will geofencing conflict with my existing schedule?

It depends on the thermostat. Some use geofencing as an override on top of a schedule; others let you choose one or the other. Check your thermostat's app settings to see which mode is active. Running both simultaneously can cause the thermostat to behave unpredictably.

What if my schedule is already irregular—is geofencing better than a fixed schedule?

Yes, that's exactly where geofencing earns its keep. A fixed schedule works well only if you leave and return at roughly the same times. If your hours vary—shift work, frequent travel, working from home some days—geofencing captures savings that a schedule would miss.

The Short Version

Turn geofencing on, set your away temperature 7–10°F from your comfort setpoint (less if you have a heat pump), confirm the app has "always on" location permission, and add any other household members' phones. Then check the history log in a few days to confirm it's actually triggering. If your house is empty for meaningful stretches on most days, you should see a noticeable reduction in how often the system runs—and that shows up on your bill within a month or two.
