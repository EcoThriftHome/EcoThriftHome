Drying Rack vs Clothes Dryer: How Much You Save Air Drying

A standard electric dryer is one of the most expensive appliances in your home. A $25 drying rack uses none of that electricity. Here's what the numbers actually look like.

~$0.60per dryer load (electric)
$100–$200potential annual savings
~6 weekspayback on a drying rack

Switching from a clothes dryer to a drying rack for most or all of your laundry can realistically save $100 to $200 per year on electricity, depending on how often you do laundry and what you pay per kWh. The rack itself costs $20–$40 and pays for itself in about six weeks. That's about as clean a math problem as you'll find in home energy savings.

That said, the real decision isn't just dollars. It's whether air drying fits your space, your schedule, and your climate. We'll get to all of that after the numbers.

What a Dryer Actually Costs to Run

A standard electric resistance dryer draws around 5,000 watts. A typical load runs about 45 minutes, so that's roughly 3.75 kWh per cycle. At the U.S. average electricity rate of about $0.16 per kWh (per EIA data), that's approximately $0.60 per load.

If you do five loads a week, that's $3.00 a week, about $156 a year, just for drying. If you're in a state with higher electricity rates (California, New York, Hawaii run $0.25–$0.35/kWh), that number climbs to $240–$340 a year for the same laundry volume.

Gas dryers are cheaper to run per load because natural gas costs less per unit of heat than electricity. A gas dryer load might cost $0.15–$0.25 in gas, though you still pay for the electricity that runs the motor, controls, and igniter. So the savings from air drying are real for gas dryer households too, just smaller.

What a Drying Rack Costs

A basic freestanding drying rack runs $20–$35. A larger, sturdier model with more bar space or a fold-flat design for storage goes up to $50–$70. There's no installation, no ongoing cost, no electricity draw. The rack just sits there and works.

A ceiling-mounted pulley rack (sometimes called an airer or clothes maid) costs $30–$80 depending on size and build quality, and uses overhead space that would otherwise go unused. Good option for smaller apartments.

If you're starting from scratch, a single standard rack pays for itself in about six weeks at five loads per week with an electric dryer. After that, the savings are just monthly cash staying in your pocket.

The Partial Switch: Usually the Right Move

Most people don't go all-or-nothing. The practical approach is to air dry the stuff that dries easily (T-shirts, underwear, socks, lightweight pants, most synthetic fabrics) and use the dryer for the things that take forever to dry indoors or benefit from the tumbling action (thick towels, heavy jeans, bed sheets).

Even cutting dryer use by 60–70% gets you most of the savings. If you currently run five loads a week and shift three of them to the rack, you're looking at roughly $90–$100 saved annually at average electricity rates, without changing anything about how you handle bulky items.

There's also a fabric-life argument for air drying. That lint in your dryer's trap? That's your clothes. Dryer heat and tumbling degrade fabric fibers over time. Air drying extends the life of clothing, which is a real but harder-to-quantify financial benefit.

The Indoor Humidity Question

This one's worth addressing directly, because it trips people up. Drying clothes indoors does release moisture into the air. In a very tight, already-humid home (say, 55%+ relative humidity regularly), that can push you into conditions where mold or mildew becomes a concern.

Worth asking before you commit to full-time indoor drying: what's your baseline indoor humidity? A $10–$15 hygrometer will tell you. If you're already running at 60% humidity in winter, adding a full rack of wet laundry isn't ideal without some ventilation or a dehumidifier running. If you're at 35–45%, as is common in drier climates or during heating season, the extra moisture from a load of laundry is essentially a non-issue and may even be welcome.

Good airflow speeds drying and manages humidity at the same time. Placing the rack near an open window in good weather, or running a ceiling fan or box fan nearby, cuts drying time noticeably and keeps moisture from sitting.

Outdoor Drying: More Savings, More Variables

If you have outdoor space, a clothesline is the fastest and cheapest way to dry clothes, and it costs almost nothing to set up. A basic clothesline and hardware runs $10–$20. Sun and wind dry clothes faster than indoor still air, and UV exposure has a mild sanitizing effect.

The obvious limits: weather, seasons, and in some places, HOA rules that ban clotheslines (yes, that's still a thing, though some states have passed laws restricting those bans). Outdoor drying is essentially free energy when the conditions cooperate. It doesn't make sense to rely on it year-round in rainy climates or cold winters, but as a supplement it's hard to beat.

What About a Heat Pump Dryer?

Heat pump dryers use about 40–50% less electricity than a standard resistance dryer, which is genuinely significant. At 5 loads a week, that could save $70–$90 a year compared to a standard electric dryer. They cost $800–$1,200 more upfront than a basic dryer, so the payback period is roughly 8–12 years, which is long but not unreasonable if you're already replacing a dryer.

We mention it here because it's the relevant middle option: if you can't or won't air dry most loads, a heat pump dryer is the next best thing. But a $25 drying rack still beats it on savings per dollar spent, by a long stretch.

Dryer-Related Savings You Can Stack

If you're keeping your dryer for some loads, a few habits reduce the cost without much effort. Cleaning the lint trap before every load keeps airflow efficient. Cleaning the dryer vent duct annually (we covered that in detail in how to clean your dryer vent duct) prevents the dryer from working harder than it needs to and is a fire safety issue as much as an efficiency one. Wool dryer balls reduce drying time by 10–25% by separating fabrics and improving airflow inside the drum, with no ongoing cost after the initial $15–$20 purchase. FAQ

How long does it take clothes to air dry indoors?

Lightweight items like T-shirts and underwear typically dry in 2–4 hours indoors with decent airflow. Thicker items like jeans or towels can take 6–12 hours. A fan aimed at the rack or a dehumidifier running nearby cuts that noticeably.

Does air drying clothes indoors raise humidity and cause mold?

It adds moisture to indoor air, which can be a real issue in tight, already-humid homes. If your indoor humidity runs above 50–55% regularly, you'd want to run a dehumidifier while drying, or open a window. In drier climates or seasons, it's usually not a problem.

Does air drying ruin clothes or make them stiff?

Air drying is actually gentler on fabric than a hot dryer. Clothes may feel slightly stiffer, especially towels. Shaking items out before hanging and giving them a brief fluff in the dryer for 5–10 minutes (no heat or low heat) solves the stiffness without much energy cost.

How much does it cost to run a dryer per load?

A standard electric dryer draws around 5,000 watts and runs about 45 minutes per load. At $0.16/kWh, that's roughly $0.60 per load. Gas dryers cost less per load but still use electricity for the motor and controls, so savings from air drying are smaller but still real.

Can renters use a drying rack?

Yes. A freestanding drying rack needs no installation and works in any apartment. It's one of the few energy-saving moves that requires zero landlord approval and costs under $30 to start. More renter-specific ideas in our energy saving tips for renters post.

The Short Version

So the three main considerations when deciding how far to take this. First: your electricity rate. The higher it is, the faster a drying rack pays back and the more you save each year. Second: your laundry volume. Five loads a week saves a lot more than two. Third: your indoor humidity. Check it before committing to full-time indoor drying; if it's already on the high side, factor in ventilation or a dehumidifier.

In short: buy the rack, use it for the easy stuff (most of your laundry), keep the dryer for bulky or wet-season loads, and you'll save $80–$150 a year with almost no effort. If you're in a high-rate state and doing a lot of laundry, that number goes higher. The rack pays for itself in weeks and keeps saving indefinitely after that.
