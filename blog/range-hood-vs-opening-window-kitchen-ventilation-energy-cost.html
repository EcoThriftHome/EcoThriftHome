Range Hood vs Opening a Window: Kitchen Ventilation Energy Costs

The electricity to run a range hood is almost nothing. The real cost is what happens to your conditioned air — and that's where the two options diverge sharply.

$1–$3Est. heating cost per open-window cooking session in winter
200–400 CFMTypical residential hood capacity

A vented range hood is cheaper to run than an open window in almost every climate and season. The fan itself uses very little electricity — roughly $6–$11 a year for typical use. The open window, meanwhile, dumps your heated or cooled air directly outside for as long as you're cooking. That's the actual cost to pay attention to.

That said, the comparison gets more nuanced depending on what type of hood you have, what season it is, and what problem you're actually trying to solve. Here's how the numbers work out.

The electricity cost of a range hood is almost irrelevant

A typical residential range hood draws somewhere between 150 and 400 watts depending on fan speed and model. Run it on medium (around 200 watts) for roughly 200 hours a year — about 25 minutes per day, every day — and you're looking at 40 kWh annually. At the U.S. average of $0.16 per kWh, that's about $6.40 a year. Even at high speed on a 350-watt motor, you're at $11.20.

That's not the number to worry about. The range hood's energy cost is essentially a rounding error on your utility bill. The real cost is in what the ventilation method does to your heating and cooling load.

What an open window actually costs you

Opening a window to clear cooking smoke or steam works, but it's blunt. You're not exhausting a narrow column of air from directly above the stove — you're exchanging a large volume of air across the entire room, or more. How much that matters depends heavily on the season.

In mild weather (say, 65°F outside, 70°F inside), opening a window for 20 minutes while cooking costs you almost nothing in heating or cooling energy. The temperature difference is small, and your HVAC barely notices.

In winter it's a different story. If it's 30°F outside and you've heated your home to 70°F, every cubic foot of air that escapes through the window has to be replaced with 30°F air that your furnace then heats back up. A kitchen window left open for 20–30 minutes while cooking can easily exchange several hundred cubic feet of air. Using rough DOE figures for natural gas at around $1.20 per therm, that replacement heating can cost $1 to $3 per session, depending on window size, drafts, and how cold it actually is. Do that most evenings through a five-month heating season and you're potentially looking at $90–$180 added to your heating bill just from cooking ventilation.

Summer works the other way: opening a window pulls in hot, humid outdoor air that your AC then has to remove. If it's 90°F outside and you crack a window for a half-hour, you're adding meaningful heat and humidity load to a system that's already working hard. This is a reasonable concern in climates where summer cooking overlaps with high AC usage. For more on the interaction between humidity and your cooling costs, our post on dehumidifiers vs air conditioners has useful context.

Ducted vs. recirculating hoods: not the same thing

Worth asking before going further: does your range hood actually vent outside, or does it recirculate air through a filter and blow it back into the kitchen?

A ducted (vented) hood exhausts air to the outdoors through ductwork. This removes heat, moisture, grease particles, and combustion byproducts. It does create a small negative pressure in the kitchen, which means your home draws in some outside air to replace what was exhausted — the same HVAC penalty as an open window, but on a much smaller scale because the volume of air moved is far less.

A recirculating (ductless) hood just runs kitchen air through a grease filter and usually a charcoal filter, then returns it to the room. It captures grease and reduces odors reasonably well. It does not remove heat or moisture. If your concern is humidity from boiling water or steam, a recirculating hood doesn't help. If your concern is cooking smells or grease particles, it does help somewhat.

The three main cost drivers to think about

So the three main things that determine whether your ventilation choice costs you money are, in order of importance:

First — the season and the indoor/outdoor temperature gap. This is by far the biggest lever. A 40°F difference (winter cooking) multiplies the HVAC penalty substantially. A 5°F difference (mild spring evening) makes the whole question almost irrelevant.

Second — how long and how often you're ventilating. A 10-minute stovetop pasta session is very different from an hour of Thanksgiving cooking with a window cracked. The cumulative effect over a whole season adds up fast if you're in a cold climate and open a window every evening.

Third — whether you have a ducted hood or a recirculating one. If your hood is ducted, it's the right tool for most of the year in most climates. If it's recirculating, it doesn't actually solve the heat and humidity problem, so opening a window alongside it might be necessary anyway — with all the associated cost.

When opening a window is the right call

There are a few specific situations where a window makes genuine sense. If you don't have any hood (vented or recirculating), opening a window is your only real option for getting steam and smoke out. If your recirculating hood just doesn't keep up with a heavy cooking session, cracking a window helps — and in mild weather, the cost is minimal.

Also worth noting: in spring and fall, when your HVAC isn't running much anyway, opening a window is effectively free ventilation. The opportunity cost is essentially zero because you're not heating or cooling the replacement air. This is probably the overlooked middle ground. If you're in a climate where you can open the kitchen window from April through October without touching the thermostat, the range hood vs. window debate matters most only in winter and peak summer.

One thing the EPA does flag in its residential cooking ventilation guidance is that gas ranges produce combustion byproducts including nitrogen dioxide. For gas stoves especially, the EPA recommends using a vented hood whenever possible, specifically to get those combustion gases out of the living space rather than just recirculating them. That's a health consideration independent of the energy cost question, but it's worth factoring in if you have a gas range.

Practical guidance by situation

If you have a ducted range hood: use it whenever you're cooking anything that generates meaningful heat, steam, smoke or grease. The electricity cost is trivial, and it's doing genuine work. Skip the window unless it's mild out and you just want fresh air.

If you have a recirculating hood: it handles grease and odor reasonably well, but doesn't address moisture or heat. For a steamy cooking session in winter, you're stuck choosing between humidity buildup or some heating penalty from a window. The better long-term fix is converting to a ducted setup if your kitchen layout allows it — the ductwork installation typically runs $200–$600, and the energy savings over time can make it worthwhile if you cook frequently.

If you have no hood at all: a window is your only option, and prioritize installing even a basic vented hood when you have the budget. A basic 30-inch ducted hood runs $80–$150 on the lower end. That's not a large spend given what it saves in HVAC energy over a few heating seasons, plus the air quality benefit on a gas range.

One more thing worth checking: if you have a ducted hood, make sure the damper (the flap inside the duct that keeps outside air from flowing back in when the hood is off) is actually closing properly. A stuck-open damper is effectively a small air leak running all the time. It's the kind of thing that quietly adds to your heating bill without anyone noticing. Similar to what we cover in air sealing crawl spaces and basements, small continuous leaks often cost more over a year than occasional larger ones.

A note on hood sizing

Oversized range hoods are a thing, and they carry their own energy downside. A 900 CFM hood running at high in a tightly sealed modern home can create enough negative pressure to backdraft combustion appliances — a safety problem — and it's hauling out far more conditioned air than necessary. ENERGY STAR and the EPA both note that for typical residential cooking, 200–400 CFM is usually sufficient. Matching hood capacity to actual cooking needs keeps the makeup-air penalty in proportion. FAQ

Does a recirculating range hood actually remove smoke and moisture?

It filters grease and some odors through a charcoal filter, but it recirculates air back into the kitchen. It does not remove moisture or heat. If humidity is your concern, a recirculating hood is not a useful tool.

How much does it cost to run a range hood all year?

Assuming 200 cooking hours per year and $0.16/kWh, a typical 200-watt range hood on medium costs about $6.40 a year. Even at high speed (350 watts), that's roughly $11. The electricity is not the concern; conditioned air loss is.

Should I open a window while cooking in winter?

Only if you don't have a vented range hood. Opening a window in winter dumps heated air outside and can easily cost $1–$3 per cooking session in heating energy, depending on how cold it is outside. A vented hood exhausts far less air volume than an open window and is the better option.

Does a range hood make my AC or heater work harder?

Yes, a ducted range hood creates negative pressure and draws in outside air to replace what it exhausts. In summer that means pulling in hot air; in winter, cold air. This is a real cost, though still much smaller than the cost of an open window for the same duration.

What CFM range hood do I actually need for home cooking?

For a standard residential gas range, 200–400 CFM is enough for typical home cooking. High-BTU burners or commercial-style ranges may need more. The EPA and ENERGY STAR note that oversized hoods on low-BTU cooktops waste energy and can create excessive makeup-air problems in tight homes.

In short

Use your ducted range hood as the default — it's cheap to run and does the job. Open a window in mild weather when you want fresh air, not as your primary ventilation strategy in winter or summer. If you only have a recirculating hood, it handles grease and odors but doesn't remove heat or moisture, so you may need a window for heavy cooking regardless. And if you have no hood at all, that's the most worthwhile upgrade to budget for.
