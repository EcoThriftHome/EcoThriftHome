How to Find Air Leaks in Your House with a Candle or Incense

A completely free, 10-minute DIY test that pinpoints exactly where your home is hemorrhaging heated or cooled air — so you know precisely where to caulk, seal, and save.

$0cost to run the candle/incense test

Somewhere in your walls, floors, and ceilings, your home is quietly leaking air like a paper bag with holes in it. You can't see those leaks, you can't hear them, and your energy bill doesn't tell you exactly where they are. But a candle or a stick of incense will.

This article walks you through a methodical, room-by-room inspection using nothing but a lit candle or incense stick, a notepad, and about an hour of your time. No special equipment, no professional needed — just physics. When warm air inside meets cold air outside, it moves. And moving air moves a flame.

Key Takeaway: A simple candle or incense test on a windy day can reveal the exact spots where your home loses conditioned air, giving you a targeted fix-list that can cut heating and cooling costs by 10–20% per year.

Why Air Leaks Are Such a Big Deal

The U.S. Department of Energy estimates that the typical American home loses a staggering amount of energy through air infiltration — gaps, cracks, and holes that collectively add up to the equivalent of leaving a window open year-round. These aren't dramatic gaping holes. They're the tiny gap where your baseboards meet the floor, the space around an old electrical outlet, the weatherstripping on your back door that has been compressed flat for a decade.

"Air sealing your home to reduce infiltration, combined with proper insulation, can save 10% to 20% on your total heating and cooling costs — representing an average savings of $200 to $600 per year."

— U.S. Department of Energy, Energy Saver: Air Sealing Your Home

That's not a rounding error. For most households, HVAC costs make up 40–50% of the total energy bill. Even shaving 10% off that category makes a meaningful dent. And unlike buying a new appliance, air sealing requires almost no capital outlay — a tube of caulk runs about $5, and a roll of weatherstripping costs $10–$20. The candle test tells you exactly where to spend that money.

What You Need Before You Start

  • A candle or incense stick — either works; incense is often easier because the smoke trail is more visible and there's less fire risk near curtains
  • A lighter or matches
  • A notepad and pen (or your phone's notes app) to record locations
  • Painter's tape — optional, but useful to mark spots on walls and trim
  • A flashlight for darker areas like attic hatches and crawl space doors
  • About 60–90 minutes on a cold or windy day

The best conditions for this test are a cold, windy day in fall or winter. The pressure difference between inside and outside is greatest then, making drafts more pronounced. If you run your exhaust fans (kitchen range hood, bathroom fan) before you start, you'll further depressurize the house and make incoming cold air even easier to detect.

How the Test Actually Works

The science is simple. Air pressure differences between inside and outside push air through any gap it can find. When that moving air passes near a lit candle or incense stick, it deflects the flame or bends the smoke. Flame bending toward a wall means air is coming in. Flame bending away from a wall means air is going out. Either way, you've found a leak.

Hold the candle or incense about 1–2 inches from the surface you're testing. Move it slowly — about 1 inch per second. Any sudden flicker or steady drift in the smoke is your signal. Mark the spot immediately before you move on.

Room-by-Room: Where to Check

1. Exterior Doors

Start at the most obvious suspects. Run the candle slowly around the entire perimeter of each exterior door: along the top (the head jamb), down both sides (the side jambs), and across the bottom. Pay special attention to the corners where the weatherstripping meets itself. Also check the keyhole and the gap around the deadbolt. The bottom of the door is one of the biggest single leak points in most homes — if daylight is visible under it, air is absolutely moving through.

2. Windows

Test every window, not just the ones that "feel drafty." Run the candle around the interior trim where it meets the wall, around the sash (the movable part), and along the sill at the bottom. Older single-pane windows often leak at the glazing putty as well — the compound that holds the glass in the frame. A slow, steady deflection there means the putty has cracked and separated from the glass.

3. Electrical Outlets and Switch Plates on Exterior Walls

This one surprises most people. Electrical boxes cut through the drywall and often have no insulation or air barrier behind them. On a cold day, you can feel cold air pouring out of outlets on exterior walls. Hold your candle directly in front of the outlet (with the plate still on) and watch for movement. Foam outlet gaskets — they cost about $2 for a pack of ten — can seal these instantly.

4. Baseboards and Floor-Wall Joints

Slowly trace the candle along the base of every exterior wall where the baseboard meets the floor. Gaps here connect directly to the wall cavity, which can communicate with the outside. This is especially common in older homes where the building has settled over decades.

5. Attic Hatch or Pull-Down Stairs

The attic hatch is one of the most energy-wasting spots in the entire house, but because it's on the ceiling, most people never think to check it. Hold the candle near the edges of the hatch. If there's no weatherstripping around it, you'll almost certainly see the flame react. Attic air in winter is essentially outdoor air — if it can reach your living space, it will.

6. Fireplace and Chimney Damper

Even with the damper closed, fireplaces are notorious air leak sources. Hold the incense at the firebox opening. A steady drift into the fireplace means the damper is seated well. A backdraft of smoke into the room suggests the flue is pulling warm indoor air upward. Also check where the chimney meets the wall or ceiling — those joints often open over time.

7. Plumbing and Wiring Penetrations

Check under every sink where pipes enter the wall. Check the dryer vent from the inside. Check anywhere that a wire or pipe punches through an exterior wall or the floor — these are almost never properly air-sealed during construction. A bead of expanding foam sealant around each penetration takes about 30 seconds and lasts for decades.

8. Recessed Lighting

If you have recessed can lights in a ceiling below an unconditioned attic, every single one is a direct hole to the attic. Hold your candle near the rim of each fixture. Any flicker is a problem. Older "non-IC-rated" cans cannot be insulated directly, but airtight covers installed from above in the attic can seal them safely.

Recording Your Findings

As you move through the house, write down every location where you detected movement. Be specific: "kitchen window, lower left corner of frame" is more useful than just "kitchen." If you have painter's tape, put a small piece at each leak location so you can find it again when it's time to seal. A phone camera is also useful — a photo of each problem spot creates a ready-made repair list.

What to Do After You Find the Leaks

Once you have your list, prioritize by severity. Spots where the flame bent strongly or smoke moved noticeably are high-priority. Spots with only a subtle flicker can wait for round two. Then match the right fix to the right gap:

  • Caulk — for stationary cracks and gaps at window frames, door frames, baseboards, and trim
  • Weatherstripping — for movable components: door edges, operable window sashes, attic hatches
  • Expanding foam — for larger irregular gaps around pipes, wires, and structural penetrations
  • Outlet gaskets — for electrical boxes on exterior walls

When to Call a Professional

The candle test is a qualitative tool — it tells you where leaks exist but not how bad they are in cubic feet per minute. If you've sealed every obvious spot and your heating bill is still stubbornly high, consider a professional blower-door test. This is what HVAC auditors use: they depressurize the entire house with a calibrated fan mounted in a doorway, then use a smoke pencil to systematically find every remaining leak. The test typically costs $150–$400 and many utility companies offer it free or at a steep discount as part of their energy efficiency programs. Check your utility's website before paying out of pocket.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the candle test for air leaks accurate?

The candle or incense test is a reliable qualitative method for detecting significant air leaks around windows, doors, and outlets. It won't measure airflow volume like a professional blower-door test, but it will identify the problem spots you need to seal — which is exactly what you need before you go buy caulk and weatherstripping.

What is the best time of year to do the candle air leak test?

Cold or windy days in fall and winter are ideal because the greater pressure difference between inside and outside makes drafts more pronounced and easier to detect with a flame or smoke. That said, if you run all your exhaust fans simultaneously during the test, you can create enough negative pressure indoors to make the test useful even in milder weather.

Is it safe to use a candle near windows and outlets?

Yes, with basic precautions. Keep the flame at least 6 inches from any curtain, fabric, or paper material. Never leave the candle unattended and always hold it — don't set it down next to a baseboard. Incense sticks are a lower-risk alternative because there is no open flame to manage; the glowing tip and smoke trail are just as effective at revealing air movement.

How much money can sealing air leaks save?

According to the U.S. Department of Energy, sealing air leaks and adding insulation can save homeowners 10–20% on total heating and cooling costs. For the average U.S. household spending around $2,200 per year on energy, that's $220–$440 in annual savings — and for poorly sealed older homes, the number can reach $600 or more.

What should I use to seal air leaks once I find them?

Match the fix to the gap type. Caulk handles stationary cracks at window and door frames. Weatherstripping handles movable components like door edges and operable window sashes. Expanding foam sealant tackles larger irregular gaps around pipes and wiring. Foam outlet gaskets handle electrical boxes on exterior walls. Between these four products — costing less than $50 total — you can address the vast majority of leaks in a typical home.
