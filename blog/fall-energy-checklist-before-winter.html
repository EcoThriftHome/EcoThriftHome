Fall Energy Checklist: 12 Things to Do Before Winter Hits

A ranked, no-fluff list of what to actually do this fall — ordered by impact and ease so you know what to skip if you're short on time.

Most of these tasks take under an hour and cost nothing or close to it. A few cost more and take longer, but they pay back within one heating season. The order below is roughly best-first: start at the top and work down as far as time allows.

1. Set a heating schedule on your thermostat

This is free and it's the highest-leverage thing on the list. The DOE estimates that dialing back 7–10°F for 8 hours a day — while you're at work or asleep — can shave around 10% off your annual heating bill. If your bill runs $1,500 a season, that's $150 back with no hardware purchase.

If you have a programmable or smart thermostat and haven't set a schedule yet, that's worth doing tonight. If you're renting and stuck with a basic dial thermostat, we have a roundup of programmable thermostats for renters that need no wiring. For more on exactly what temperatures to target, see our post on the best winter thermostat temperature to save money.

2. Replace or clean the HVAC filter

A clogged filter makes your furnace or heat pump work harder, which raises your bill and shortens the equipment's life. Most 1-inch filters should be swapped every 1–3 months; thicker 4–5-inch media filters can go 6–12 months. Check the label on the filter currently installed — if it looks grey and dense, replace it now before the heating season starts in earnest.

A clean filter can improve HVAC efficiency by up to 15%, according to the DOE. At $8–$15 for a standard filter, that's about the best return on a purchase you'll find. We cover the full replacement schedule in our HVAC filter guide.

3. Schedule an HVAC tune-up

This is the one item worth paying a professional for. A furnace or heat pump that hasn't been serviced in a year or more can have issues — a corroded ignitor, a cracked heat exchanger, low refrigerant charge — that you simply can't diagnose yourself. Catching those problems in October is far cheaper than dealing with a no-heat call on the coldest night of the year, when HVAC companies charge emergency rates and have week-long backlogs.

A tune-up typically runs $80–$150. Ask the technician to check the heat exchanger, test ignition, verify the flue is clear, and confirm the thermostat is calibrated correctly.

4. Weatherstrip exterior doors

Hold your hand near the bottom and sides of an exterior door on a cold day. If you feel airflow, the weatherstripping is gone or compressed past usefulness. A full door weatherstrip kit costs about $12–$20 and takes 30 minutes to install. If the gap at the bottom is the main problem, a door sweep (also called a door bottom seal) handles that for around $10–$15.

5. Seal window gaps with caulk or rope caulk

Exterior window frames develop small gaps where the frame meets the wall, especially in older homes where wood has expanded and contracted over decades. A $6 tube of paintable silicone caulk handles permanent gaps. For rental situations where you need something removable, rope caulk (also called window glazing cord) pulls off cleanly in spring and costs about $5 per roll.

These aren't the same as full window insulation kits with film — just sealing the perimeter gaps is faster and handles most of the problem. If your windows are genuinely drafty through the glass itself (single-pane, rattling sashes), our post on insulating windows without replacing them covers the film kit option in detail.

6. Check and seal attic hatch and pull-down stairs

This one surprises people. An uninsulated attic hatch — that plain drywall square in a hallway ceiling, or the pull-down stair opening — loses heat almost as fast as a small window left open all winter. The hatch itself is usually just a piece of drywall with no insulation and no weatherstripping.

A foam cover for a standard hatch runs about $30–$50. For pull-down stair openings, tent-style covers start around $60. We ran the full payback math in our attic hatch cover post — the short version is that most covers pay for themselves in one heating season in a cold climate.

7. Reverse your ceiling fans to winter mode

Most ceiling fans have a direction switch — usually a small toggle on the motor housing. In winter, you want the blades spinning clockwise at low speed. That pushes warm air that's pooled near the ceiling back down along the walls without creating a wind-chill effect.

This doesn't save a lot on its own, but it costs nothing and takes about 30 seconds per fan once the fan is off. Worth doing every fall. The full explanation of why direction matters is in our ceiling fan direction guide.

8. Drain and store garden hoses; shut off exterior faucets

This isn't directly an energy-saving task, but a burst pipe from a frozen exterior faucet is an expensive problem that tends to happen when the house is cold — meaning while you're trying to save money on heat. The fix is simple: disconnect hoses, drain them, and shut off the interior valve that feeds outdoor spigots (most homes built after the 1980s have one). Takes five minutes.

9. Insulate the water heater and hot water pipes

If your water heater is in an unheated basement or garage, it's working harder than it needs to all winter. A pre-cut water heater blanket ($25–$35) can reduce standby heat loss by 25–45% on older tank heaters, according to the DOE — worth roughly $20–$45 per year on a typical water heating bill. Skip the blanket on newer heaters that already have thick factory insulation; check whether the outer shell feels warm to the touch, which is the sign that it's worth adding one.

Foam pipe insulation for the first 3–6 feet of hot water pipe out of the heater costs about $5 and adds another small improvement. We covered the full math in how to insulate your water heater.

10. Seal electrical outlets and switch plates on exterior walls

Foam gaskets that go behind outlet and switch covers on exterior walls cost about $3–$5 for a 10-pack. They're a 2-minute installation and they block a surprisingly real source of cold air infiltration in older construction where the insulation wasn't well sealed around electrical boxes. It's not a dramatic saving, but it's one of the highest return-on-time tasks on this list. We wrote up exactly how to do it in our guide to insulating electrical outlets and switch plates.

11. Check fireplace damper and consider a chimney balloon

An open fireplace damper is roughly like leaving a 12-inch hole in your ceiling all winter. Even closed dampers on older fireplaces tend to seal poorly. If you don't use the fireplace regularly, a chimney balloon (also called a fireplace draft stopper) plugs the flue from inside and costs about $50–$70. It's inflatable, removable in seconds, and dramatically cuts cold-air downdraft and heat loss through the flue.

If you do use the fireplace occasionally, just make it a habit to verify the damper is fully closed after each use. The full case for chimney balloons is in our post on chimney draft stoppers.

12. Do a quick audit of your basement rim joists

The rim joist is the band of framing that sits on top of your foundation wall, right where the floor framing starts. It's one of the most under-insulated spots in most homes — often just exposed wood or fiberglass batts that have shifted over time, with cold outside air infiltrating through the gaps. You can seal and insulate rim joists yourself with 2-inch rigid foam cut-to-fit and spray foam around the edges, for around $1–$2 per linear foot of perimeter.

This one takes a few hours and some modest material cost, so it belongs at the end of the list. But if you've done everything above and want to keep going, rim joists are where most of the remaining air leakage is hiding in a typical basement. We have a full walkthrough in our basement rim joist insulation guide.

How to prioritize if you're short on time

So the three things that need to happen before anything else, in order of urgency:

First — set the thermostat schedule, because it's free and starts saving immediately. Second — replace the HVAC filter and book the tune-up, because those protect expensive equipment. Third — weatherstrip exterior doors and seal window gaps, because those are low-cost fixes with payback measured in weeks, not years.

Everything else on the list is worth doing, but if you only have one weekend, start there.

Frequently asked questions

When should I start the fall energy checklist?

Ideally before temperatures drop consistently below 40°F in your area — usually September or October depending on climate. HVAC tune-ups book up fast once cold weather arrives, so schedule that one first.

How much can fall weatherization save on a heating bill?

The DOE estimates that air sealing and insulation together can cut heating and cooling costs by up to 20%. The exact number depends on how leaky your home is to start with, but most homes have enough gaps that the free and low-cost fixes here will pay for themselves in one season.

Do I need to hire someone for all of this?

Most of the 12 items on this list are DIY. The one worth paying for is the HVAC tune-up, since a technician can catch problems — cracked heat exchanger, low refrigerant, corroded ignitor — that you can't spot on your own.

What's the single most impactful thing on this list?

It depends on your home, but for most people it's either the thermostat setback schedule (free, saves $100–$180/year) or sealing the biggest air leaks around doors and windows (under $30 in materials). Both have near-instant payback.

In short: do the thermostat and filter this week, book the HVAC service, then spend a Saturday on weatherstripping and caulk. That covers most of your heating savings before the first really cold month hits.
