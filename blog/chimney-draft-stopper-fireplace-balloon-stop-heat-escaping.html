Chimney Draft Stopper: How a Fireplace Balloon Stops Heat Escaping (and Saves You $150/Year)

Picture this: you've sealed every window gap, added weatherstripping to your front door, and even tucked foam behind your electrical outlets. Smart moves, all of them. But if your home has a traditional wood-burning fireplace that you rarely (or never) use, there's a gaping hole in your insulation strategy that could be costing you $100–$200 every single heating season — and you can fix it in about five minutes for less than $50.

We're talking about your chimney. Even when the damper is closed, a conventional fireplace flue is far from airtight. Warm, conditioned air rises and escapes continuously, and cold outside air drafts back down to replace it. A chimney draft stopper — most commonly sold as a fireplace balloon or chimney pillow — seals that opening simply, safely, and reversibly. Here's everything you need to know to choose, size, install, and get the most out of one.

Key Takeaway: Inserting a chimney balloon into an unused fireplace flue is one of the highest-return, lowest-effort weatherization upgrades available to homeowners, with a typical payback period of just one heating season.

Why Your Chimney Is Basically a Hole in Your Roof

A traditional masonry or factory-built fireplace is engineered to move combustion gases up and out of your home — which means it's designed to allow airflow. The damper plate is intended to block that flow when the fireplace isn't in use, but most cast-iron dampers warp, corrode, and fail to seat properly over time. Even a brand-new damper has air gaps around the frame by design; it's not a sealed unit.

"Fireplaces can be a significant source of energy loss in homes. An open damper on a fireplace can let as much heat escape as leaving a window open." — U.S. Department of Energy, Energy Saver Guide

Research from the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory has found that fireplaces — especially those with damaged or poorly-seating dampers — can account for 8–14% of a home's total heating loss. For a household spending $1,400/year on heating (the U.S. EIA national average for gas heat), that's $112–$196 disappearing up the flue annually.

The stack effect makes things worse. Warm air naturally rises, creating a pressure differential that pulls heated indoor air toward any opening near the top of the house. Your chimney sits at the highest point and has the widest unobstructed opening in the entire building envelope. It's the path of least resistance.

What Is a Fireplace Balloon (Chimney Draft Stopper)?

A chimney balloon — sometimes called a chimney pillow, flue blocker, or chimney draft stopper — is a thick, laminated plastic bag that you insert into the flue throat (just above the damper plate, inside the firebox) and inflate using a small plastic straw or hand pump. As it expands, it presses firmly against all four sides of the flue, creating a near-airtight seal.

The two most common materials are:

  • Multi-layer laminated polyethylene film — lightweight, easy to pack away, and effective for most standard flue shapes.
  • Polyurethane foam-filled pillows — slightly more rigid, conforming more easily to irregular masonry surfaces.

Both types include a small inflation tube that hangs below the damper and into the firebox — this serves as both the inflation valve and a visual reminder tab. Most manufacturers print a bright warning label on the tab that reads something like "REMOVE BEFORE LIGHTING FIRE." Some models also include a low-melting-point safety feature: if the balloon is accidentally left in place when a fire is started, the material softens and deflates before it can cause a hazard.

Choosing the Right Size: Measure Before You Order

This is the step most people skip and then regret. Chimney balloons come in standard sizes, and buying the wrong one means either a poor seal or an inability to inflate it inside the flue. Here's how to measure correctly:

  1. Open the damper fully. Using a flashlight, look up into the flue throat — the narrowed section just above the smoke shelf.
  2. Measure the width (side to side) and depth (front to back) of the flue opening at the throat, not at the firebox opening itself.
  3. Note the shape — most are rectangular, but some older masonry fireplaces have rounded or oval flues.
  4. Choose a balloon that is 1–2 inches larger than your measured dimensions on each side. The balloon needs room to press outward and create a seal.

Common sizes include 9"×9", 9"×12", 12"×12", 12"×15", and 15"×15". If your flue is an unusual size, some manufacturers sell custom-cut foam pillow versions, or you can stack two standard balloons side by side for very wide openings.

Step-by-Step Installation (It Really Takes 5 Minutes)

  1. Make sure there's no fire, hot embers, or ash in the firebox. Let it cool completely.
  2. Close the damper. Reach up and confirm it's fully shut — you'll be inserting the balloon just above it.
  3. Partially inflate the balloon outside the fireplace to about 50% capacity so it holds a shape but is still pliable.
  4. Slide the balloon up through the firebox opening and position it in the flue throat, above the damper. The inflation tube should hang down into the firebox where you can reach it.
  5. Continue inflating until the balloon presses firmly against all four sides of the flue. You should feel noticeable resistance on the straw.
  6. Pinch the valve closed, or use the included clip, and tuck the inflation tube so the warning tag is clearly visible hanging in the firebox.
  7. Stand in front of the fireplace and check for drafts with a stick of incense or a lighter flame. The smoke should not pull toward the firebox opening.

To remove before a fire: simply pull the inflation tube, allow the balloon to deflate, and slide it down and out. Store it in the provided bag for next season.

Important Safety Rules — Don't Skip These

A chimney balloon is extremely safe when used correctly, but the consequences of misuse are serious. Follow these rules without exception:

  • Always remove the balloon before lighting any fire. It should be completely out of the flue before you strike a match. No exceptions.
  • Keep the warning tag visible. Never tuck it behind the damper or push it out of sight. If guests or family members might use the fireplace, tell them about it.
  • Check the balloon monthly for deflation. A slow-leaking balloon that's partially deflated won't seal and may drop into the firebox. Give the inflation tube a quick squeeze each month and top it off if needed.
  • Do not use in a gas fireplace. Gas fireplaces require continuous ventilation for safe combustion and pilot light operation. A draft stopper is only appropriate for wood-burning fireplaces that are not in use.
  • If you have a gas-log insert in a wood-burning fireplace, consult a chimney professional before installing any blocker, as ventilation requirements vary by appliance.

DIY Alternative: Rigid Foam Board Insert

If you want to try a no-cost solution first, a piece of 2-inch rigid polyisocyanurate foam board (polyiso) cut to fit the flue throat and wrapped in foil tape can work reasonably well. Cut it 1 inch larger than the measured opening on each side, score the edges so they compress slightly, and push it up into the flue. Wrap the edges in a few layers of foil tape to help it grip.

The downsides: it's harder to get a truly airtight fit, it's awkward to remove and reinstall, and if the foam drops into a lit fireplace (because someone forgot it was there), it creates a chemical smoke hazard. For most people, the $30 commercial balloon is worth it for the better seal, easier installation, and built-in safety reminder.

FAQ: Chimney Draft Stoppers

What is a fireplace balloon or chimney draft stopper?

A fireplace balloon (also called a chimney pillow or chimney draft stopper) is an inflatable device inserted into the flue opening of an unused fireplace. It expands to create an airtight seal that prevents warm indoor air from escaping up the chimney and stops cold outside air from drafting down.

Is it safe to use a chimney balloon when you have a fireplace?

Yes, provided you remove it before lighting a fire. Most chimney balloons include a pull-tab reminder system and are made from heat-sensitive materials that deflate if accidentally left in place when a fire is lit, acting as a safety fail-safe. That said, always make removal a deliberate step in your fire-starting routine.

How much can a chimney draft stopper save on my energy bill?

Estimates from the U.S. Department of Energy suggest an open damper or unsealed chimney can account for 8–14% of a home's heating loss. Sealing it with a chimney balloon can save roughly $100–$200 per year for an average U.S. household, depending on climate, home size, and fuel costs.

What size chimney balloon do I need?

Measure the width and depth of your flue opening (just inside the firebox, above the damper) before purchasing. Most standard fireplaces use a 9"×9" or 9"×12" balloon, but rectangular and larger sizes are available. When in doubt, go one size up — balloons can be slightly under-inflated to fit smaller openings.

Can I make a DIY chimney draft stopper?

Yes. A common DIY option is a rigid foam insulation board cut to size and wrapped in foil tape. While cheaper upfront, it lacks the airtight seal of a properly inflated balloon and must be custom-fitted carefully. For most homeowners, a $25–$50 commercial balloon delivers better performance and is far easier to install and remove.

If your fireplace sits unused for most of the year — or even all of it — a chimney draft stopper is one of the smartest $30–$55 purchases you can make before winter arrives. The savings are real, the installation is trivially easy, and the only ongoing effort is a monthly squeeze-check and remembering to pull it out before you light a fire. Stack this with proper damper maintenance, weatherstripped doors, and a programmable thermostat, and you're building a genuinely tight, efficient home without a major renovation budget.

Start with a tape measure, find your flue size, and order before the first cold snap hits. Your heating bill will thank you by February.
