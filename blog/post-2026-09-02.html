Power outages are no longer rare inconveniences—they're a growing, measurable risk. Whether it's wildfire-related rolling blackouts, hurricane season, or aging grid infrastructure, the question for most homeowners has shifted from "Do I need backup power?" to "Which backup power system actually makes financial sense?" That's exactly the question we set out to answer.

Solar generators—portable battery stations that can be charged by solar panels, wall outlets, or car adapters—have matured significantly. Battery chemistry has improved, prices have dropped, and capacities that once cost $3,000 are now available for under $1,000. But marketing claims are everywhere, so we cut through them with hard numbers: actual usable watt-hours, real-world recharge times, cost per watt-hour, and projected lifespan.

Key Takeaway: For most homeowners, a 1,000–2,000 Wh LiFePO4 solar generator paired with 200–400W of solar panels provides 1–2 days of essential backup at a total cost of $700–$1,800—far cheaper per cycle than a gas generator when you factor in fuel and maintenance.

What to Look for Before You Buy

Before diving into specific models, here are the five numbers that actually matter—and why marketing specs can be misleading:

  • Usable Wh vs. rated Wh: Most lithium NMC batteries only let you use 80–90% of rated capacity. LiFePO4 batteries typically allow 90–95%. A "1,000 Wh" NMC unit may deliver only 850 Wh in practice.
  • Battery chemistry: LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate) lasts 2,000–3,500 cycles versus 500–1,000 for NMC. If you plan to use this more than once a year, LiFePO4 wins on lifetime cost.
  • Continuous AC output (watts): This determines which appliances you can run simultaneously. A 1,000W output won't run a window AC unit (1,400W) or a microwave (1,200W).
  • Max solar input (watts): Higher solar input means faster recharge time. A 400W input recharges a 1,000 Wh battery in about 2.5 hours; 100W input takes 10+ hours.
  • Cost per usable Wh: Divide the purchase price by usable watt-hours. Anything under $0.80/Wh is competitive; under $0.60/Wh is excellent.

Solar recharge from a 500W panel array gets you from 0–80% in under 1.5 hours—among the fastest in this class.
