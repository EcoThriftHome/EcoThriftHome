Wall Outlet Foam Gaskets vs Child Safety Plugs for Drafts: Which One Actually Works?

Both fit behind or into your wall outlets, but only one meaningfully blocks cold-air infiltration. Here is the honest, data-backed breakdown before you spend a dime.

Every October, the same question pops up in home-improvement forums: "I can feel cold air coming from my wall outlets — should I grab those little plastic plug covers or the foam gaskets?" It sounds like a small question, but the answer matters for both your energy bill and, if you have young kids, for safety. Spoiler: the two products solve almost entirely different problems, and confusing them is a very common — and very fixable — mistake.

Key Takeaway: Foam gaskets seal the air gap around the electrical box and are the correct tool for stopping outlet drafts; plastic child safety plugs cover only the tiny slot openings on the outlet face and provide negligible draft reduction.

Why Outlets Are a Real Source of Drafts

Before comparing the two products, it helps to understand exactly where the cold air is coming from. A standard electrical box — whether it holds an outlet or a light switch — is essentially a small open-sided box punched through your drywall. On exterior walls, there is often no insulation directly behind the box, and the gap between the box edges and the drywall cutout is rarely caulked. That creates a direct pathway: outdoor air sneaks into the wall cavity, travels through the box, and flows right into your living space through the slots in the outlet face or around the edges of the wall plate.

The DOE has flagged electrical outlets and switch plates on exterior walls as a meaningful contributor to residential air infiltration, particularly in homes built before 1980 when air-sealing standards were essentially nonexistent. In a blower-door test — the gold-standard diagnostic for home air leakage — exterior outlets reliably show up as pressure-failure points.

"Sealing air leaks throughout your home — including around electrical outlets and plumbing penetrations — can reduce your heating and cooling costs by up to 20 percent, making it one of the highest-return investments a homeowner can make."

— U.S. Department of Energy, Energy Saver Guide

That 20% figure is for whole-home air sealing (attic bypasses, rim joists, plumbing penetrations, and so on), not outlets alone. But outlets are a simple, no-tool-required piece of that larger puzzle — and at roughly $0.25 each, the return on investment is nearly instantaneous.

Foam Gaskets: What They Are and How They Work

An outlet foam gasket is a pre-cut piece of expanded polyethylene foam — the same lightweight, closed-cell foam used in padded envelopes — shaped to fit behind your standard wall plate. You unscrew the wall plate, slip the gasket over the outlet or switch, then reinstall the plate. The gasket compresses against the drywall and creates a continuous seal around the entire perimeter of the electrical box cutout.

This is the critical point: the gasket seals the perimeter gap, not the outlet slots themselves. That perimeter gap is typically ¼ inch to ½ inch wide all the way around the box — a surprisingly large total opening when you add it up across a dozen exterior-wall outlets. Foam gaskets address that gap directly and do so for pennies per outlet.

Installation is genuinely easy. You do not touch a single wire. As a precaution, flip the circuit breaker for the outlet you are working on. Unscrew the single center screw on the wall plate, slide the plate off, press the gasket in place (it is self-locating — the cutouts line up with the outlet prong slots), and reinstall the plate. Done. Under two minutes from start to finish.

Child Safety Plugs: What They Actually Do

Plastic plug-in outlet covers — the little caps that press into the prong holes of an unused outlet — were designed with one purpose: to prevent curious toddlers from inserting metal objects into live electrical contacts. They are a legitimate and inexpensive child-safety tool. What they are not is an air-sealing product.

Here is why: the outlet slot openings that a safety plug covers represent a tiny fraction of the total air-leakage area at an outlet. The standard NEMA 5-15 duplex outlet has two sets of slots, each roughly 1 inch × 0.1 inch. Combined, that is less than 0.4 square inches of open area in the outlet face. Compare that to the perimeter gap around the electrical box — easily 4 to 6 square inches of open pathway on an older installation — and you can see the mismatch. Plugging the slot holes while leaving the perimeter unsealed is like taping over the keyholes while leaving the door ajar.

Child safety plugs also have a practical safety downside that has driven most electricians and the Consumer Product Safety Commission to recommend against basic plug-in caps in favor of tamper-resistant outlets (TROs). TROs have spring-loaded internal shutters that only open when equal pressure is applied to both slots simultaneously — exactly what a plug does — making them far more reliable than caps that children can learn to remove.

How to Prioritize Your Outlets

Not every outlet in your house warrants a gasket — interior wall outlets have almost no air-sealing value because there is no exterior air pathway behind them. Here is how to triage the job:

  1. Exterior walls first. Walk around the perimeter of your home's main living area. Any outlet or switch plate on a wall that faces the outdoors is a candidate. In a typical 1,500 sq ft ranch home, this might be 10–20 outlets and switches.
  2. Feel for drafts. On a cold, windy day, hold the back of your hand near the outlet. Even a subtle cool sensation confirms air movement. A stick of incense held nearby will show smoke deflection if airflow is significant.
  3. Older homes, higher priority. Pre-1980 construction almost universally lacks outlet-box sealing. Post-2000 construction with foam-sheathed walls may already have this addressed at the framing stage, but it is still worth checking.
  4. Don't forget switches. Light switch plates on exterior walls are just as leaky as outlet plates and use the same gasket form factor. Most packs include both outlet and switch gasket shapes.

The Right Combination for Families with Young Children

If you have toddlers in the house, the answer is not foam gaskets or safety plugs — it is foam gaskets plus tamper-resistant outlets. Install the gasket behind the plate for air sealing, and specify tamper-resistant outlets (they are required by the National Electrical Code in all new residential construction since 2008) for child safety. If replacing outlets feels like too big a project right now, slide-plate safety covers — which require a deliberate sideways motion to expose the slots — are a better stopgap than basic plug caps because young children find them far harder to defeat.

Expected Savings: Keeping It Real

Outlet gaskets alone will not slash your utility bill in half. Anyone who tells you otherwise is overselling. What the evidence does support is this: outlets and switch plates are one of many small air-leakage sites that, together, add up to a significant whole-house problem. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory has estimated that the total uncontrolled infiltration in an average U.S. home is equivalent to leaving a window open year-round. Outlet sealing is one fast, cheap piece of closing that metaphorical window.

A realistic estimate for a 1,500 sq ft home in a cold climate (Zone 5–6): sealing 15 exterior-wall outlets and switches with foam gaskets might reduce your annual heating spend by $10–$30 per year on its own. At a total material cost of roughly $4–$8 for a pack of gaskets, that is a payback period measured in months — and the gaskets last for decades. Scale that alongside other air-sealing work (attic hatch weatherstripping, rim joist foam, door sweeps) and the cumulative savings become genuinely meaningful.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do wall outlet foam gaskets actually save energy?

Yes, but modestly on their own. Foam gaskets seal the gap between the electrical box and the drywall, blocking conditioned air from escaping into wall cavities. The DOE estimates that sealing all electrical outlets and switches in an older home contributes to overall air-sealing savings of up to 20% on heating and cooling bills when combined with other measures like attic and rim-joist sealing.

Do plastic child safety plugs stop drafts at outlets?

Only marginally. Plastic plug-in covers block the small slot openings in the face of the outlet, but they do nothing to seal the larger air gap around the electrical box behind the wall plate. For draft control, they are far less effective than foam gaskets.

Can I use both foam gaskets and safety plugs together?

Absolutely, and it is the recommended approach for homes with young children. Install the foam gasket behind the wall plate for air sealing, then use tamper-resistant outlets or slide-cover safety caps on the outlet face for child safety. The two solutions address different problems and work well together.

Which outlets should I prioritize for foam gaskets?

Focus first on outlets and switch plates located on exterior walls, especially in older homes built before 1980 that lack insulated wall cavities. These are the biggest sources of outlet-related drafts. Outlets on interior walls have a much smaller impact on energy loss.

Is installing foam gaskets a DIY job?

Yes. You do not need to touch any wiring. Simply turn off the circuit breaker for the outlet as a precaution, unscrew the wall plate, press the pre-cut foam gasket over the outlet or switch opening, then reinstall the plate. The whole process takes under two minutes per outlet.

Bottom Line

If your goal is to stop cold air from pouring through your exterior-wall outlets this winter, foam gaskets are the right tool — full stop. They are cheap, fast to install, effective, and permanent. Plastic safety plugs are a child-safety product being misapplied as an air-sealing product, and they fall well short of the job. Grab a 36-pack of foam gaskets, spend a Saturday afternoon working through your exterior walls, and you will have checked off one of the easiest and fastest-payback air-sealing tasks on any homeowner's list. Then, if you have young children, follow up with tamper-resistant outlets for actual electrical safety.
