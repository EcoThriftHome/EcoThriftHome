Dehumidifier vs Air Conditioner: Which Uses Less Electricity?

A dehumidifier draws a fraction of the power, but it also heats the room. Here's how to tell which one your house actually needs on a given day.

A dehumidifier uses far less electricity than an air conditioner. A typical portable dehumidifier pulls 300 to 500 watts. A window AC pulls 700 to 1,200, a portable AC around 1,100, and a central system somewhere near 3,000 while the compressor is running. Hour for hour, the dehumidifier costs a third to a sixth as much to run.

That doesn't make it the cheaper way to get comfortable, though, because the two machines aren't doing the same job. One removes heat from your house. The other removes water and quietly leaves the heat behind. Which one saves you money depends on whether your problem is temperature or moisture, and most people can't tell the difference by feel.

The difference between a dehumidifier and an air conditioner

Inside, they're nearly the same machine: a compressor, refrigerant, a cold coil and a hot coil. Air blows across the cold coil, water condenses out of it, and the water drips into a pan or bucket.

The difference is where the hot coil sits.

  • An air conditioner puts its hot coil outside. Heat gets pumped out of the house, so the indoor air comes back both cooler and drier.
  • A dehumidifier keeps both coils in the same box, in the same room. The air gets chilled to pull the moisture out, then immediately reheated by the hot coil. It comes out drier, and a little warmer than it went in.

That second point surprises people, so it gets its own section.

Yes, a dehumidifier makes the room warmer

Water vapor carries hidden heat. Engineers call it latent heat, and it's the same energy it took to evaporate that water in the first place. When a dehumidifier condenses the vapor back into liquid, that energy is released as ordinary heat you can feel. Add the electricity the unit itself uses, which also ends up as heat, and the room warms up.

How much? A 50-pint unit running flat out removes roughly a liter of water an hour. Condensing a liter releases about 0.68 kWh of heat. Add about 0.5 kWh of electricity and you're putting around 1.2 kW, or about 4,000 BTU per hour, into the room. That's in the range of a small space heater. In a closed bedroom you'll see the thermometer climb a few degrees.

The air isn't carrying much more total energy than before; most of that heat was already in the room as humidity. It just changed from the kind you feel as stickiness to the kind you feel as warmth. Whether that's a good trade depends on the temperature you started at.

When the dehumidifier is the right call

The dehumidifier wins any time the air is cool enough but wet. A few situations where that's reliably true:

  • Mild, muggy weather. Upper 60s to mid 70s with humidity over 60% feels clammy, but there isn't much heat to remove. A dehumidifier fixes the clammy part for a third of what the AC would cost.
  • Basements and crawl spaces. They stay cool on their own and almost always run damp. Running the whole-house AC harder to dry out a basement is the expensive way to do it.
  • Rooms you don't cool. Closets, garages, laundry rooms and storage rooms where you care about mold, rust or musty smells, not comfort.
  • Spring and fall in humid climates. In places like Houston or New Orleans, the AC isn't optional from June through September. The shoulder months are different. A 72-degree morning with the humidity at 85% is exactly where a dehumidifier earns its keep.

When you need the air conditioner

If the room is already warm, a dehumidifier makes it worse. Past about 78 degrees indoors, adding a box that releases 4,000 BTU an hour into the room is the opposite of what you want.

  • Hot afternoons. When it's both hot and humid, the AC handles both. It already pulls a lot of water out of the air as part of cooling; that's what the drain line is for.
  • Bedrooms at night. Most people sleep better in the high 60s to low 70s. A dehumidifier can't get you there.
  • Running both at once. This can make sense in a damp house, but know the cost: every watt the dehumidifier uses becomes heat your AC then has to pump back out.

Check your AC before buying anything

A lot of "my house feels sticky even with the AC on" complaints aren't a dehumidifier problem at all. Three things to rule out first:

  • Thermostat fan set to ON instead of AUTO. With the fan running constantly, water that collected on the cold coil gets blown right back into the house between cooling cycles. Switching to AUTO is free and often makes a noticeable difference.
  • An oversized system. An AC that's too big for the house hits the temperature setpoint fast and shuts off before it's run long enough to pull much moisture. You get a cold, clammy house. Lower fan speed settings or a thermostat with a dehumidify function can help.
  • Dirty filter or coil. Restricted airflow hurts both cooling and moisture removal.

What about AC "dry mode"?

Many window units and most mini-splits have a dry or dehumidify mode. It runs the fan slowly so the coil stays very cold and wrings more water out of the air, and the compressor cycles more gently than in cool mode. It usually uses less power than cool mode, though how much less varies by model.

The useful difference: unlike a standalone dehumidifier, dry mode still dumps its heat outside. You get the drying without the room warming up. If you already have a mini-split or window unit in the room, try dry mode before buying a separate dehumidifier.

Dehumidifier vs portable AC

If you're shopping for one or the other for a single room, the portable AC is the less efficient choice of the two by a wide margin. Single-hose models exhaust indoor air outside, which pulls warm, humid outdoor air back in through every gap in the house. If the room is hot, a window unit is the better buy. If it's only damp, a dehumidifier is.

Whole-house dehumidifier vs air conditioner

Whole-house units tie into your ductwork and typically draw 600 to 900 watts. They make the most sense in tightly sealed newer homes and in humid climates where the AC alone can't keep indoor humidity down during mild weather. They're an add-on to central air, not a replacement for it.

Target humidity and a $10 tool that settles the question

The EPA recommends keeping indoor humidity between 30% and 50%, and below 60% at minimum to discourage mold and dust mites. A cheap digital hygrometer shows you whether you're actually over that line. It turns the decision into something simple:

  • Under 76°F and over 55% humidity: dehumidifier, or AC dry mode if you have it.
  • 76–80°F with moderate humidity: ceiling fan, and the AC set a few degrees higher than usual.
  • Over 80°F: air conditioner. Let it handle the moisture too.
  • Basement, closet or crawl space: a dedicated dehumidifier on a humidistat, set to 50%.

If you're buying one

Look for the ENERGY STAR label and check the Integrated Energy Factor (IEF), which is liters of water removed per kWh. Higher is better. ENERGY STAR currently requires at least 2.01 L/kWh for 25 to 50-pint portable units and 3.30 for 50 pints and up. Get a built-in humidistat so it shuts itself off, and a drain hose connection if it's going in a basement. A unit you have to empty twice a day ends up unplugged.

Frequently asked questions

Does a dehumidifier use less electricity than an air conditioner?

Yes. A portable dehumidifier typically draws 300 to 500 watts, while a window AC draws 700 to 1,200 watts and a central system around 3,000 watts while running. Per hour, a dehumidifier costs roughly a third to a sixth as much.

Is it cheaper to run a dehumidifier or an AC all day?

The dehumidifier, by a wide margin, if the room is cool enough that you only need to remove moisture. If the room is hot, the dehumidifier won't make it comfortable and will add heat, so the AC is the only thing that works.

Does a dehumidifier make a room warmer?

Yes. It releases the heat stored in the water vapor it removes, plus the heat from its own electricity use. A 50-pint unit at full output adds roughly 4,000 BTU per hour to a room, similar to a small space heater.

Is AC dry mode cheaper than cool mode?

Usually, yes. Dry mode runs the fan slower and cycles the compressor more gently, so it typically uses less power than cool mode. Unlike a standalone dehumidifier, it still sends heat outside, so the room doesn't warm up.

How much electricity does a dehumidifier use per month?

A 50-pint unit drawing 500 watts for 10 hours a day uses about 150 kWh a month, around $24 at 16 cents per kWh. With a humidistat cycling it off once the room reaches 50%, real-world use is often half that.

Can a dehumidifier replace an air conditioner?

Only when temperature isn't the problem. A dehumidifier makes a mild, damp room feel much better, but it can't lower the temperature, so it can't replace an AC in hot weather.
