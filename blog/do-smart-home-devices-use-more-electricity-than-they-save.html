Do Smart Home Devices Use More Electricity Than They Save?

Every smart device draws standby power around the clock. Here's which ones earn that back — and which ones are just adding to your bill.

1–5WTypical smart device standby draw
~$50/yrEst. smart thermostat savings (ENERGY STAR)
$1–$7/yrAnnual standby cost per device at $0.16/kWh

Most smart home devices do save more electricity than they consume — but only the right ones, used the right way. A smart thermostat with a real schedule running your heat pump will pay back its standby draw many times over. A smart bulb in a lamp you turn on manually every evening and rarely dim? It's quietly adding a little cost with nothing much to show for it.

The standby draw of these devices is real and worth knowing. It's just rarely the main event. The question is whether the savings the device enables are large enough to outrun that always-on power consumption. For some devices, that's an easy yes. For others, it genuinely depends on how you use them.

Why Every Smart Device Has a Standby Cost

Smart devices stay connected to your Wi-Fi or a hub 24 hours a day so they can respond to schedules, voice commands, and app requests. That connectivity requires power even when the device isn't actively doing anything. It's the same reason a router draws electricity whether anyone is browsing or not.

The draw is small — usually 1 to 5 watts depending on the device — but it runs continuously. At the U.S. average of roughly $0.16 per kWh, a device drawing 2 watts around the clock costs about $2.80 per year. That's not alarming on its own. The issue is when you have a dozen of these devices and none of them are actually reducing energy use elsewhere in the house. That's when the standby load starts to matter.

Worth checking: before buying any smart device for energy savings, ask what it's supposed to control and whether that thing would otherwise be wasting energy without the smart device. If the answer is no, the device is a net cost.

Smart Thermostats: The Clear Winner

If you're trying to justify smart home devices on pure energy math, a smart thermostat is where to start. ENERGY STAR estimates savings of around $50 per year on average for homes that use a programmed schedule. That's a conservative figure; homes with longer unoccupied periods, colder climates, or older setback habits can see considerably more. The thermostat's own standby draw at 1–2 watts costs under $3 per year, so you're looking at a ratio of roughly 15:1 or better in favor of savings.

The catch — and it's a real one — is that the savings only materialize if you actually program it. A smart thermostat set to hold a constant temperature does almost nothing a cheap programmable thermostat wouldn't do. The value is in geofencing (auto-setback when you leave), scheduling, and learning features that trim runtime during hours when no one's home. We've written more about this in our post on smart thermostat habits that save $180 per year.

Smart Plugs: Useful on the Right Appliances

A smart plug draws 1–2 watts in standby and costs roughly $1.40–$2.80 per year just to sit in the wall. That cost is easy to beat if the appliance it controls has significant vampire power or runs longer than it needs to.

Good candidates: entertainment centers, gaming consoles, home office setups, window AC units you forget to turn off. These are things that draw meaningful power when idle or when no one's using them — often 10–50 watts in standby. A smart plug on a home theater receiver that draws 20 watts in standby, used to cut power when you go to bed, saves roughly $28 per year. That's a solid return on a $12–$20 device.

We'd skip the smart plug on the fridge, only because a refrigerator never actually turns off — there's nothing to schedule or cut. Same logic applies to anything that runs a compressor continuously. The plug adds standby load with no offsetting savings. For more on identifying which appliances have real standby draw, see our post on tracking vampire power by appliance.

Smart Bulbs: It Depends Heavily on How You Use Them

Smart bulbs are where the math gets genuinely unfavorable for a lot of households. The issue isn't the light energy — a smart bulb and a standard LED use roughly the same wattage when they're on. The issue is that smart bulbs draw 0.5–1 watt continuously as long as the wall switch is live, so they can listen for commands. If you have six smart bulbs in a room and never use the app or scheduling, you're paying an extra $4–$8 per year in standby for no benefit.

The case for them improves if you're actually using scheduling (automatically off at midnight, dim to 20% after 9 PM) or if the bulbs replace fixtures you habitually leave on too long. In a room where you regularly forget to turn lights off, a schedule or motion-triggered rule can recover real money. But in a fixture you're already diligent about, a standard LED does the same job for less money and zero standby draw.

There's a separate comparison worth reading if you're deciding between the two: smart bulbs vs LED bulbs.

Hubs, Voice Assistants, and the Infrastructure Question

A smart hub running continuously at 3 watts costs about $4.20 per year. That's worth it if the hub is orchestrating schedules and automations that actually reduce energy consumption — say, coordinating thermostat setbacks, turning off lights in unoccupied rooms, and cutting standby power on entertainment devices. It's not worth it if it's mostly running smart lights you control manually.

Voice assistants are a different story. A device like a smart speaker draws 2–4 watts continuously whether or not it's controlling anything energy-related. If you're using it mainly for music, timers, and weather checks, it's a net energy cost. It's not a large one, but it's a pure add. Voice control of thermostats and lights can be convenient, but convenience and energy savings aren't the same thing.

The Three Situations Where Smart Devices Are a Net Cost

So the three main situations where smart devices reliably cost more than they save:

First — the device controls something that was never wasting energy in the first place. A smart plug on a lamp you always turn off manually. Smart bulbs in a fixture with an occupant who's already diligent. There's no savings to capture, so the standby draw is pure waste.

Second — the device is set up but not configured. A smart thermostat on "hold" at a single temperature. Smart bulbs with no schedule or automation. The device adds standby draw but delivers none of the behavioral savings it was designed for.

Third — you're running too much infrastructure for too little gain. A hub, a bridge, two voice assistants, and a router extension all running 24/7 to control four light bulbs. The infrastructure standby load can exceed the savings from the devices it's managing.

How to Know What Your Devices Are Actually Drawing

The honest answer to whether your specific setup is net positive is to measure it. A plug-in power meter will tell you exactly what any device draws in standby. Plug the smart device in, leave it for a few hours, check the watt reading. If it's drawing 3 watts and you have no automation set up, you're paying about $4 a year for nothing. If it's drawing 1 watt and it's cutting 20 watts of standby on a cable box every night, you're well ahead.

FAQ

How much standby power does a smart home hub use per year?

Most smart hubs draw 2–4 watts continuously. At 3 watts and $0.16/kWh, that's about $4.20 per year — noticeable but not a dealbreaker if the hub is managing devices that actually save energy.

Do smart plugs waste electricity just by being plugged in?

Yes, but only a small amount. A typical smart plug draws 1–2 watts in standby, costing roughly $1.40–$2.80 per year each at $0.16/kWh. That cost is worth it if the device it controls would otherwise run continuously or be difficult to remember to turn off.

Are smart bulbs more efficient than regular LED bulbs?

No. Smart bulbs use the same amount of light energy as equivalent LEDs when on, but they also draw about 0.5–1 watt in standby whenever the switch is live. If you use them with the wall switch off, they can't respond to app commands and you lose that standby draw — but then there's no point in having them. The honest comparison is in our smart bulbs vs LED bulbs post.

Does a smart thermostat always save more than it costs to run?

Usually, yes — but only if you actually set it up with a schedule or geofencing and your HVAC system runs enough hours to benefit. ENERGY STAR estimates roughly $50 per year in savings. The thermostat itself draws only about 1–2 watts, so its own standby cost is under $3 per year. The savings math depends on your climate and current habits.

What smart devices are not worth it from an energy standpoint?

Smart bulbs in fixtures you rarely dim or schedule, smart plugs on appliances that never really turn off (like refrigerators), and voice assistants used mainly for music and timers rather than energy control. The device adds standby draw without meaningful savings in those cases.

How do I find out how much standby power my smart devices are using?

A plug-in power meter like the Kill A Watt will tell you exactly what any device draws. Plug the smart device into the meter, leave it overnight, and check the watt-hour reading. It costs about $25 and pays for itself quickly if it helps you identify devices drawing more than expected.

The Short Version

Smart thermostats and smart power strips on entertainment or office setups are genuinely worth it on energy math alone. Smart plugs earn their keep on devices with real standby waste, and do nothing on devices that run continuously. Smart bulbs are only net positive if you actually use the scheduling or automation features; otherwise a standard LED does the job better and cheaper. Voice assistants and hubs are infrastructure costs that need to be justified by what they're managing.

In short: measure what your devices draw, verify that each one is set up to actually reduce consumption elsewhere, and don't count on "smart" to mean "efficient" by default.
